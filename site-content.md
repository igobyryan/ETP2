# Foundations

## Foundations

Communities around the world have proven that ecological and social restoration is possible (even under the toughest conditions) by tackling root causes and leveraging small but powerful changes.

This tool teaches you how EcoTipping Points work and the systemic factors needed to create conditions for an EcoTipping Point. We illustrate through the experience of communities who faced collapse, but reversed it through well chosen ecological interventions they were capable of pulling off. It is the shared patterns of these case studies that shapes our understanding of what an EcoTipping Point is and how to make it happen. Just as they were successful in setting off a chain of events that created systemic change, turning vicious cycles into virtuous cycles, each of their stories have set off a chain of changes for other communities who borrowed their choices, modifying them to their own conditions, to bring back their fish, farms, and healthy forests and water.

#### 1. What are Tipping Points?

Tipping points are when changes reach a threshold that sets in motion systemic change.

#### 2. How do Tipping Points create systemic change?

When changes leverage enough force to begin a vicious or virtuous cycle.

#### 3. Why are vicious and virtuous cycles key to systemic change?

They reinforce, amplify and trigger change across a system.

#### 4. How can communities craft effective positive tipping point levers?

Community members develop a shared understanding of the problem, then brainstorm targeted, high-impact actions capable of reversing those cycles.

#### 5. How does tipping point success at one location spread to other locations?

Success spreads to other locations when people elsewhere learn about the success and decide to try it for themselves.

#### 6. How does local tipping point action contribute to the resolution of global problems such as Climate Change?

The success of tipping point levers at a local level show that it's not necessary to wait for action from national governments or international accords to solve global problems.

# Ingredients for Success — Overview

Ingredients for Success

## Essential Elements for Making Things Better

Assessment Questions: Evaluate whether the essential ingredients for success are present in your community and situation.

##### Ingredients for Success Tool

### About This Tool

Across the communities where ecological restoration has taken hold, certain conditions show up repeatedly. Some are social: shared awareness of the problem, committed local leadership, the capacity to work through resistance. Others describe the relationship between community and ecosystem: memory of how things once were, diversity that provides options, a willingness to let nature recover on its own terms. EcoTipping Points calls these the Ingredients for Success.

The ten ingredients aren't a checklist to complete before you can act. They're a diagnostic lens. Some will be present in your situation; some will be weak or absent. Knowing which is which shapes what's possible and where to put your energy.

#### What This Tool Does

Each ingredient page helps you assess whether that condition exists in your community, identify strategies for building on what's already there, and decide how to respond when something critical is missing. Some ingredients need attention from the earliest fieldwork onward; others are most useful once your community has candidate interventions to test in Reverse It.

### The Ten Ingredients

Each ingredient includes guidance on how to recognize it, amplify it if present, build it if absent, or work around it when necessary.

01

#### Outside Stimulation and Facilitation

Communities that can draw on outside knowledge and support (without ceding control) move further faster.

02

#### Shared Community Awareness and Commitment

A community's shared understanding of a problem and how to address it, including collective ownership and commitment.

03

#### Harmony Between Community and Ecosystem

Restoration efforts must work with both natural systems and community needs, creating mutual benefit that sustains itself.

04

#### Enduring Commitment of Local Leadership

Restoration takes years. Communities that succeed have people willing to stay committed through skepticism, setbacks, and slow progress.

05

#### Letting Nature Do the Work

Ecosystems know how to recover. The most powerful strategies don't fight nature; they get out of its way.

06

#### Mobilizing Community Commitment

Using powerful symbols and rapid results to build and maintain community engagement.

07

#### Overcoming Social Obstacles

Every restoration faces resistance. Communities that succeed find ways through it with strategy, solidarity, and persistence.

08

#### Social and Ecological Diversity

Diversity is insurance. Communities and ecosystems with more variety have more options and paths forward when conditions change.

09

#### Social and Ecological Memory

The past holds blueprints. Communities and ecosystems that can draw on what worked before have a head start.

10

#### Building Resilience

Success doesn't protect itself. Communities that build resilience are prepared for disruptions that will come.

#### Using Ingredients with Systems Thinking

Several ingredients matter throughout the process, from the earliest fieldwork through years after an intervention is chosen. Shared community awareness and strong local leadership are like this. They're already shaping the work in Recognize It, and they keep mattering long after Lock It In.

Once you reach Reverse It, they sharpen your choices too. After your community identifies candidate interventions, use them to evaluate what's already in place to give an intervention real traction, and what's missing that could undermine it.

# Ingredient: Outside Stimulation and Facilitation

Ingredient

## Outside Stimulation and Facilitation

Communities that can draw on outside knowledge and support move further faster.

##### Ingredients for Success Tool

↓ Download as PDF

A printable version of this tool to take into the field.

##### Related Ingredients

→ Shared community awareness and commitment
→ Social and ecological memory
→ Enduring commitment of local leadership

Communities facing environmental decline often need access to things they can't generate entirely from within: technical knowledge, demonstrated examples from elsewhere, facilitation skills, connections to legal authority or funding. Outside partners can provide these. But outside support only works when the community stays in control of its own decisions. The most effective outside partners don't arrive with a plan. They arrive with tools, questions, and a commitment to follow the community's lead. The tipping point lever comes from inside the community; outside support is what helps the community reach it on its own terms.

### Recognize It

- Has the community been able to connect with outside partners (NGOs, researchers, government extension workers) who listen before they prescribe?
Outside engagement that begins with understanding the community's situation, rather than proposing solutions, is the foundation of effective support. How an outside partner shows up in early contact tells you a lot about whether they'll be useful later.

- Has outside contact helped the community see connections between its problems and the wider environmental or social system?
One of the most valuable things outside knowledge can offer is a systemic lens: helping a community understand why things are the way they are, not just what to do about it. This kind of understanding builds the community's own analytical capacity, not dependence on outside diagnosis.

- Has the community had access to demonstrated examples (other places where similar interventions have worked)?
Seeing what's possible elsewhere (a healthy reef, a restored watershed, a working agroforestry farm) is often more persuasive than any explanation. When communities can visit these places and talk directly with the people who built them, they return with conviction rather than just information.

- Is outside support strengthening the community's own decision-making capacity, or substituting for it?
The test isn't whether outside partners are involved; it's whether their involvement leaves the community more capable of directing its own future. Outside partners who short-circuit community deliberation, or who move at a pace that prevents genuine consensus, undermine the very commitment they're trying to build.

- Has outside support helped the community access resources, legal recognition, or networks it couldn't reach alone?
Sometimes the most valuable outside contribution isn't expertise but connection: to government recognition, legal protections, funding streams, or other communities with relevant experience. These are things communities often can't access without help, and accessing them doesn't require ceding control.

### Amplify It

If outside support is already present, these strategies help the community get more from it.

##### Set the terms of engagement clearly

Communities that are explicit about what they need from outside partners (and what they don't) get better support. This means naming the community's decision-making process upfront, being clear about what kinds of input are welcome and what kinds aren't, and redirecting outside partners who start to overstep. A community in Ecuador with strong consensus-based institutions found that NGOs who couldn't adapt to their pace created conflict rather than progress; the community's process was right, and the outside partner needed to adjust.

##### Use site visits and peer exchanges strategically

Direct exposure to places where similar interventions have worked is one of the highest-value things outside support can provide. When the Apo Island fishermen visited a working no-fish reserve and saw the results for themselves, they came back as advocates. Peer exchanges (community to community, practitioner to practitioner) are often more credible and more immediately practical than expert-to-community knowledge transfer.

##### Demand capacity-building, not just service delivery

Outside support that includes skills training, development of local leadership, and strengthening of community institutions builds capacity for making things better. Outside support that does things for communities without know-how transfer creates dependency. Communities should expect (and ask for) the former.

##### Use outside networks to connect with other communities

Outside partners often have access to networks of communities working on similar problems. These connections are valuable not just for technical knowledge but for solidarity, for shared problem-solving, and for the credibility that comes from learning from peers rather than experts.

##### Sustain the relationship through difficult phases

Outside support is most valuable not just at the beginning, when momentum is high, but during the hard middle: when early results are slow, when obstacles arise, when community energy risks stalling. Communities should look for outside partners willing to maintain engagement over time, not just provide an initial push.

### Build It

If the community lacks access to outside support, these strategies can help establish it.

##### Reach out actively; don't wait

Outside support rarely arrives without invitation. Local leaders, community organizations, or individuals with wider connections can reach out to universities, NGOs, government services, or faith-based organizations. Knowing what the community is looking for (facilitation skills, specific technical knowledge, connections to legal or funding resources) makes the search more focused and the engagement more productive from the start.

##### Start with information-sharing, not intervention

Access to information about how other communities have addressed similar problems, or about how the local ecosystem works, can shift what a community believes is achievable, without requiring any outside organization to take a directing role. Invited speakers, community visits, or facilitated discussions with outside experts can serve this function while keeping the community in the lead.

##### State the boundaries before the work begins

When laying the groundwork for a partnership, be specific about what you're asking a partner to do, and what you're asking them not to do. Say up front if you need them to stay out of the community's own decisions. It's harder to walk a partner back once they've already started making calls that weren't theirs to make.

##### Engage government programs and university partnerships

Agricultural extension services, university research programs, and regional development initiatives are often underused sources of outside support. They may not arrive perfectly suited to community needs, but they can be engaged and shaped. Universities in particular often have students and researchers eager for field partnerships on community-defined terms.

##### Connect with peer communities first

A community that has already done what you're trying to do is often the most useful and credible source of outside support. They've navigated the same obstacles, and their success is direct evidence that what you're attempting is achievable. Facilitating peer connections (visits, ongoing exchanges) can be as valuable as any expert engagement, and carries none of the hierarchical baggage.

### Work Around It

##### When outside partners push their own agenda rather than following community direction

Name it directly and early. A community that understands what good outside support looks like can redirect partners who are substituting their judgment for community judgment. If a partner can't adapt to the community's pace and process, it may be better to disengage than to allow their agenda to displace community ownership. The cost of a misaligned outside partner is often higher than the cost of working without one.

##### When outside support is unavailable or unreliable

Some communities are too remote, too small, or too disconnected to easily attract outside support. In these cases, prioritize what the community already knows and can do, drawing on social and ecological memory, while looking for any available outside connection, however modest. A single person with relevant outside knowledge, engaged informally, can provide meaningful stimulation if the community approaches the relationship thoughtfully.

##### When past outside interventions have damaged trust

Communities that have been burned by outside organizations (promises made and broken, projects that disrupted community life without making things better, partners who left when funding ran out) have earned their skepticism. Rebuilding trust requires a different kind of outside presence: slower, more transparent, more honest about limitations, and more willing to follow community direction. Small, concrete actions that deliver on what they promise are more effective than large commitments. The community sets the pace.

# Ingredient: Shared Community Awareness and Commitment

Ingredient

## Shared Community Awareness and Commitment

A community's shared understanding of a problem and how to address it is essential for effective action.

##### Ingredients for Success Tool

↓ Download as PDF

A printable version of this tool to take into the field.

##### Related Ingredients

→ Enduring commitment of local leadership
→ Overcoming social obstacles

A community's shared understanding of a problem and how to address it is essential for effective action, including a common recognition of the issue and its causes, a shared vision of possible solutions, a collective sense of ownership over decisions, and a united commitment to follow through. While outside ideas can inspire action, it is ultimately the community's collective experience, decision-making, manpower, and financial resources that drive meaningful progress.

### Recognize It

The checklist below will help you to recognize what facets of shared community awareness and commitment you already have, so that you can amplify what exists, and either build or work around what does not exist.

- Do you have a formal or informal body for addressing your crisis that involves stakeholders from various parts of the community?
Having community members with a range of skills and experience at the decision making table takes advantage of the whole community's knowledge. It also broadens support for actions that require substantial commitment and sacrifice, and provides the financial and labor resources to carry out the actions.

- Do your decision makers have a process for choosing actions that all can agree upon and participate in carrying it out?
A real sense of collective ownership of decisions is needed for the risks involved in taking action, and the commitment of time, labor or financial resources needed to carry it out. A strong decision making body provides ways for all members to contribute, commits to finding common ground, and supports those members taking the greatest risks to their livelihood, so that they can participate.

- Do your decision makers have a shared understanding of what the problem is and how it occurred?
Choosing a course of action requires a shared vision of the nature and source of the crisis, so that actions are directed at core problems. Gathering the knowledge, memories, and experience of the community allows decision makers to map the events that triggered the vicious cycle of decline.

- Has your group determined key actions that, if successfully carried out, may be powerful enough to reverse the cycle of decline?
Many changes are possible, but not all are powerful enough to set change in motion. By mapping which elements of your vicious cycle are changeable, and brainstorming what can be done to change that element, it becomes easier to see which actions are both doable and potentially impactful enough. Any action that does not have enough force is just swimming against the current.

- Is the community committed to the action?
Although ideas stimulating action may come from outside, the community needs to move forward with its own decisions, manpower, and financial resources.

### Amplify It

If you have elements of shared community awareness and commitment in place, nurture and amplify them; they are central to success.

##### Increase the diversity of your stakeholders

Having stakeholders from various parts of the community increases the chances of success. It brings a breadth of knowledge, memory, and experience to the table. It also broadens support for decisions that require substantial commitment and sacrifice, and provides the financial and labor resources to carry them out.

##### Arrive at decisions based on consensus

Disagreement is normal in community decision-making. Procedures that help find common ground without loss of integrity matter here, as do avenues for those with dissenting opinions to support agreements for shared action. Consensus doesn't require unanimity; it requires that no one is left with objections serious enough to undermine follow-through.

##### Map your vicious cycle of decline together

Turning around ecological decline requires a lever with enough force. Doing the mapping process as a group (using the ETP systems thinking process) helps the community arrive at a shared understanding of what triggered the crisis, what elements are changeable, and which actions may be forceful enough to reverse the cycle. A shared map is harder to walk away from than an analysis handed down from outside.

##### Have all decision makers contribute manpower, financial support, or other resources

Direct participation in carrying out decisions strengthens engagement while providing the funds and labor needed to succeed. This requires planning inclusive strategies for contribution so that everyone has a functional role. Some community members will face greater sacrifice than others; developing ways to support those taking the greatest risks makes it possible for them to follow through.

##### Build in flexibility

A course of action will need modification as results come in, as resource requirements become clearer, or as new obstacles emerge. Agreed-upon benchmarks and procedures for reevaluating and modifying shared decisions build flexibility into action plans. This matters most when field conditions don't match what was expected.

### Build It

Shared community awareness and commitment is essential for turning around ecological decline. If you don't yet have it, these strategies help build the elements most likely to matter.

##### Interview community members who have specialized knowledge about the crisis

There are community members whose experience is essential to understanding the problem: scientists, educators, elders, those working the land, those managing household resources. Reaching out to pool that experience creates a foundation for mapping problems and solutions. It also creates a base of people now involved who may have the commitment to join a decision-making effort.

##### Assemble a group to map your vicious cycle of decline

The ETP systems thinking process uses knowledge pooled from the community to identify what triggered decline, what elements in the vicious cycle are changeable, and which may be forceful enough levers to reverse it. Doing this as a group produces shared understanding of the problem and potential solutions, which is harder to generate any other way.

##### Start with whoever is already committed, however few

In most communities, some people are more frustrated with the status quo or more willing to act than others. Starting there, supporting early efforts and building visible results, creates the proof of concept that draws others in. Visible progress comes first; broad commitment follows.

##### Use early wins to build shared ownership

When initial actions produce visible results, make sure the community sees them together. Shared witness of improvement (a reef visibly recovering, a field producing more, water returning to a dry channel) builds a collective narrative of "we did this" that is far more motivating than any explanation. Plan for early results that skeptics can see and verify.

### Work Around It

Shared community awareness and commitment is essential for reversing ecological decline and hard to work around when absent. When full community commitment isn't achievable, these strategies help move forward without it.

##### Focus on the action forceful enough to turn things around

When broad consensus isn't possible, concentrate collaboration on the single action most likely to reverse the vicious cycle rather than trying to build agreement across the full scope of the problem. A smaller coalition committed to a high-leverage intervention can produce visible results that eventually bring skeptics along.

##### Set differences aside around shared material interest

Ideology, values, and even reasons for wanting change are common obstacles to collaboration. When those differences block progress, refocus the group on the material conditions everyone shares: the fishery that feeds all families, the water supply that all households depend on, the soil that all farms require. Shared stakes in a concrete resource often create more durable cooperation than shared values.

##### Work with willing community members and document results

If some community members won't participate, start with those who will. Careful documentation of what the smaller group achieves (before and after evidence of recovery, economic data, testimony from participants) builds the case that draws in those who were initially unwilling. Results are a more persuasive argument than any framing.

##### Acknowledge the limits honestly

If commitment is thin, avoid overpromising what the effort will achieve. Communities that understand why commitment matters, and what its absence means for the odds of success, can make better decisions about whether and how to proceed. Honest framing of what's needed and what's at stake is itself a form of respect for community judgment.

# Ingredient: Harmony Between Community and Ecosystem

Ingredient

## Harmony Between Community and Ecosystem

When social organization fits the ecosystem it depends on, both begin to heal together.

##### Ingredients for Success Tool

↓ Download as PDF

A printable version of this tool to take into the field.

##### Related Ingredients

→ Shared community awareness and commitment
→ Letting nature do the work
→ Building resilience

Making things better happens when a community builds or revives the social organization needed to care for its ecosystem, and the ecosystem responds by better meeting community needs. Rules, institutions, and practices evolve to match what the land or water actually requires. As ecosystem health improves, it reinforces the social commitment that made restoration possible. Social and environmental gains go hand in hand. At the heart of this process is what researchers call "social commons for environmental commons": organization tailored to managing a community's shared ecological resources.

### Recognize It

The checklist below will help you recognize what elements of social-ecological harmony you already have, so that you can amplify what exists, and either build or work around what does not.

- Does your community have formal or informal rules governing how people use shared natural resources?
Rules about fishing, forest use, water rights, or land management (even informal ones) are the foundation of social-ecological harmony. Their existence signals that the community already understands it has shared stakes in a shared resource.

- Are those rules based on how the ecosystem actually works, rather than external regulations imposed from outside?
Rules that come from community knowledge of local conditions tend to be more practical to enforce and more effective in practice. Outside regulations often miss the specific dynamics of a local ecosystem.

- Do the people who depend most directly on the ecosystem have a voice in how it's managed?
Fishers, farmers, herders, and water users have detailed knowledge of how their ecosystem behaves. When their experience is incorporated into management decisions, those decisions are more likely to fit the system they're managing.

- Is there a shared understanding that community wellbeing and ecosystem health rise and fall together?
The recognition that healthy fisheries mean food security, that healthy forests mean water supply, that healthy soil means family income: this shared understanding is what motivates people to accept the costs and constraints of collective management.

- When the ecosystem improves, does the community notice and connect it to their actions?
Visible feedback (more fish, cleaner water, better harvests) closes the loop between community behavior and ecological response, reinforcing both the rules and the social institutions that produced the improvement.

### Amplify It

If elements of social-ecological harmony are already in place, here are strategies for strengthening them.

##### Strengthen the fit between your rules and your ecosystem

If management rules exist but outcomes are mixed, look carefully at whether the rules match the way the ecosystem actually functions. Overfishing limits set without understanding fish reproduction cycles, or water restrictions that don't account for seasonal variation, will underperform even with strong community commitment. Incorporate ecological knowledge (from scientists, from elders, from direct observation) into refining how the rules work.

##### Make the connection between social and environmental gains visible

When ecosystem recovery produces tangible community benefits (more fish, better water, higher yields) document and communicate those connections explicitly. The more clearly people can see that their collective management choices are producing results, the stronger the social institutions become. Before/after documentation, community site visits, and shared celebration of visible recovery all reinforce the feedback loop.

##### Evolve your social institutions as the ecosystem changes

A social commons that fits your ecosystem today may need adjustment as conditions change: whether from climate variability, successful recovery that changes what management is needed, or new external pressures. Build in regular opportunities for the community to reassess whether their rules and institutions still fit the system they're managing.

##### Connect management authority to those doing the managing

When the people who enforce the rules are the same people who depend on the ecosystem, compliance tends to be higher and enforcement more effective. Decentralized, community-based management outperforms top-down regulation in most EcoTipping Point stories; not because government is unimportant, but because local authority aligned with local stakes creates stronger accountability.

### Build It

If social-ecological harmony doesn't yet exist, here are strategies for creating it.

##### Start with the ecosystem's actual needs

Before designing management systems, spend time understanding how your ecosystem works: what drives its decline, what conditions it needs to recover, what pressures it can absorb and what it cannot. This ecological understanding is the foundation for building social organization that fits.

##### Revive or adapt traditional institutions

Many communities have historical forms of collective management that were abandoned or undermined: traditional water councils, fishing agreements, forest stewardship practices. These institutions often encode generations of ecological knowledge about local conditions. Where they still exist in memory, adapting them to current conditions is frequently more effective than building new institutions from scratch.

##### Create new institutions modeled on the ecosystem's logic

Where traditional institutions don't exist or can't be revived, design new ones that reflect how the ecosystem functions. The Apo Island marine sanctuary succeeded in part because the boundary of community management matched the actual boundaries of the reef ecosystem the community depended on. Form follows ecological function.

##### Ensure those who bear the costs also share the benefits

Harmony between community and ecosystem breaks down when the costs of conservation fall on one group while the benefits flow to another. Build benefit-sharing into your management structure from the beginning: whether through equitable water rights, shared fishing revenues, or community ownership of ecotourism income.

### Work Around It

When social-ecological harmony is difficult to establish, these strategies can help navigate common obstacles.

##### When external authority undermines local management

Government regulations, outside commercial interests, or national protected area designations can override community management systems, sometimes unintentionally. If this is a barrier, focus first on documenting your community's management practices and outcomes; evidence of success is the strongest argument for local authority. Look for legal frameworks, co-management agreements, or recognition of indigenous resource rights that can formalize community control.

##### When the ecosystem is too degraded to respond quickly

If the ecosystem has been so severely depleted that initial management efforts show little result, community resolve can erode before the social-ecological feedback loop has a chance to form. In this situation, pair conservation rules with active restoration (replanting, habitat rehabilitation, species reintroduction) to accelerate the ecosystem's response and create visible early wins that reinforce the social institutions.

##### When there's no history of collective resource management

Some communities have individualized resource use so thoroughly (through privatization, outside takeover, or breakdown of traditional practices) that collective management feels foreign. Start with a small, defined resource that the community agrees has shared stakes. Early success with a limited commons builds the trust and institutional experience to expand.

# Ingredient: Enduring Commitment of Local Leadership

Ingredient

## Enduring Commitment of Local Leadership

Restoration takes years. The communities that succeed have people willing to stay committed through the skepticism, the setbacks, and the slow work of proving it can be done.

→ Mobilizing community commitment with powerful symbols and rapid results

Community ecological restoration doesn't happen in a single season. It requires sustained effort over years: navigating early skepticism, managing setbacks, negotiating with outside authorities, and keeping people engaged through the long period between commitment and visible results. Local leadership is what makes that sustained effort possible. Not necessarily a single charismatic individual, but people within the community (formal or informal leaders, council members, women's groups, early adopters) who remain committed to the work and credible enough to bring others along. The most durable leadership grows from the community's own institutions and expands over time as success creates new leaders.

### Recognize It

- Does the community have individuals or groups who have consistently advocated for restoration, even when others were skeptical or disengaged?
Early commitment in the face of doubt is one of the clearest indicators of leadership quality. People who maintained their position through the difficult early period (before results were visible) have demonstrated the persistence that long-term restoration requires.

- Are community leaders trusted and credible with the people they're trying to bring along?
Technical knowledge and formal authority matter less than trust. Leaders who are seen as genuinely part of the community (who share its risks and benefits, who have a track record of following through) are far more effective at building the broad commitment that restoration requires than outside-appointed or self-appointed advocates.

- Does leadership extend beyond a single individual?
Restoration that depends entirely on one person is fragile. When that person leaves, gets sick, loses interest, or faces personal setbacks, the whole effort is at risk. Distributed leadership (spread across a council, a women's group, multiple early adopters, a network of practitioners) is more resilient than individual leadership, however capable.

- Have leaders been willing to persist through conflict with outside authorities?
Many restorations eventually come into conflict with outside interests: government agencies, commercial operators, neighboring communities, national laws that don't account for local conditions. Leaders who have navigated those conflicts, rather than backing down or being bought off, have demonstrated the kind of commitment that durable restoration requires.

- As restoration has proceeded, has leadership expanded to include new people?
The best indication that local leadership is healthy is that it grows. Early adopters become trainers. Community members who were initially skeptical become advocates. The whole community takes on a leadership role in spreading what it has learned to others. This expansion of leadership is both a sign of success and a condition for continued success.

### Amplify It

If committed local leadership is already present, these strategies help develop and sustain it.

##### Invest in developing the next generation of leaders

The people who started a restoration won't always be there to finish it. Deliberately bringing younger community members into leadership roles (through training, through mentorship, through giving them visible responsibility) ensures that commitment outlasts any individual. Ask: who will carry this forward in ten years, and what do they need now to be ready?

##### Expand the definition of leadership beyond formal roles

Village councils and elected leaders matter, but so do informal leaders: respected elders, women who organized a cooperative, farmers who were first to try something new and became local proof that it works. Recognizing and supporting these informal leaders broadens the base of commitment and makes the restoration more resilient to any single person's departure.

##### Create structures that keep leaders accountable to the community

Leadership that is accountable to the community it serves is more durable than leadership that operates independently of it. Regular community meetings with a genuine review function, transparent reporting on outcomes, and mechanisms for community members to raise concerns all keep leadership grounded in shared purpose rather than personal interest. Concrete accountability mechanisms help here too: regular project meetings, and officers, appointed or elected, who check that meeting notes and financial records are accurate.

##### Celebrate and document what leaders have accomplished

Visible recognition of leadership (within the community and beyond it) reinforces commitment and builds the kind of reputation that attracts outside support and inspires other communities. When Khao Din's leaders hosted visitors from other villages, and when Punukula became a destination for thousands of people learning about NPM, the recognition of what those communities had built deepened the commitment of the leaders who had built it.

##### Support leaders when they face outside pressure

Leaders who take on conflict with outside authorities (governments, commercial interests, powerful neighbors) need to know the community is behind them. That backing, when it's genuine and visible, is what allows leaders to negotiate from a position of strength rather than isolation.

### Build It

If committed local leadership is absent or weak, these strategies can help develop it.

##### Start with whoever is already committed, however few

Leadership doesn't have to be broad to begin. In most communities, there are one or two people who are more curious, more frustrated with the status quo, or more willing to try something new than others. Starting there (supporting their early efforts, helping them demonstrate results) creates the proof of concept that draws others in. The first farmer in Punukula who tried NPM became the evidence that made it possible for others to follow.

##### Look for leadership in unexpected places

Formal leadership structures don't always produce the most committed leaders for restoration work. Women, young people, and community members without official status have often been the most effective drivers of change, because they have the most to gain, because they're less invested in the status quo, or because they're trusted in ways that formal leaders aren't. Don't assume leadership capacity maps onto formal authority.

##### Use early wins to build leadership credibility

Nothing builds a leader's credibility faster than visible results. Prioritizing interventions that produce tangible benefits quickly (within the first growing season, the first year) gives committed leaders something concrete to point to when skeptics push back. Results are the most persuasive leadership tool available.

##### Help leaders connect to peer networks beyond the community

Leaders who can learn from and compare notes with people doing similar work in other communities are more effective and more resilient than those operating in isolation. Facilitating those connections (through visits, exchanges, regional networks) expands leaders' knowledge, validates their commitment, and gives them allies when things get hard.

### Work Around It

##### When formal leaders are opposed to restoration or captured by outside interests

Work with whoever in the community shares the commitment (informal leaders, women's groups, youth organizations) and build the base of evidence and support that eventually makes official opposition untenable. This takes longer, but leadership that emerges from demonstrated community benefit is more durable than leadership that depends on formal authority.

##### When previous leaders have left and no one has stepped into the gap

Don't assume leadership is absent just because it isn't visible. In most communities, there are people with the capacity to lead who haven't yet been asked, haven't seen a reason to step forward, or haven't had the support to do so. Creating visible opportunities (a community meeting with a genuine agenda, a small early project with clear responsibilities) often surfaces leadership that wasn't apparent before.

##### When a single leader has carried everything and is burning out

Burnout in key leaders is one of the most common causes of restoration stalling or failing. Address it directly: redistribute responsibility, bring in support, create structures that share the load. And treat it as a signal that the leadership base needs to be wider; a restoration that depends on one person's heroic effort is not yet sustainable.

# Ingredient: Letting Nature Do the Work

Ingredient

## Letting Nature Do the Work

Ecosystems know how to recover. The most effective restoration strategies don't fight nature; they get out of its way, and then work with what comes back.

##### Ingredients for Success Tool

##### Related Ingredients

→ Social and ecological memory
→ Building resilience
→ Harmony between community and ecosystem

Every ecosystem carries within it the biological design for its own recovery. Given the right conditions (reduced pressure, restored habitat, returning species), nature mobilizes self-organizing processes that no human intervention could replicate at the same scale or complexity. The most effective restoration strategies work with these processes rather than trying to substitute for them. This doesn't mean doing nothing. It means understanding how the local ecosystem works, identifying what's preventing natural recovery, removing those barriers, and then designing human interventions that align with and build on what nature is already trying to do.

### Recognize It

- Has the ecosystem shown any signs of natural recovery when pressure is reduced, even temporarily?
Patches of forest regrowing after a field is left fallow, fish returning to an area where nets haven't been used recently, soil recovering where chemical inputs were stopped: these are signs that ecological memory is intact and that the ecosystem retains the capacity for self-restoration. Even small signs are significant.

- Are there natural processes at work in the ecosystem that could do more of the restoration work if supported?
Water moving through a landscape, predator-prey relationships regulating pest populations, nitrogen-fixing plants building soil fertility, mycorrhizal networks connecting tree root systems: natural processes that are already operating, however weakly, can often be strengthened rather than replaced. Identifying which processes are present and which are blocked is the first step.

- Does the proposed intervention mimic or align with how the local ecosystem naturally functions?
Interventions that work with the grain of the ecosystem (polyculture farming that mimics forest structure, earthen dams that direct natural water flow, protected areas that allow fish populations to recover through their own reproduction) tend to be more effective and more self-sustaining than those that impose a different logic on the landscape.

- Would removing the key pressure allow significant natural recovery to begin?
Sometimes the most effective intervention is subtraction rather than addition: stopping the destructive fishing, ending the chemical pesticide application, halting the encroachment on a recovering forest. When ecological memory is intact, removing the main source of disruption can trigger recovery processes that are far more complex and comprehensive than anything a community could engineer directly.

- Are natural processes doing work that would otherwise require expensive infrastructure or inputs?
Underground water transport through an aquifer, natural pest control by predatory insects and birds, soil fertility maintained by organic matter cycling: these are services that nature provides for free when ecosystems are functioning. Recognizing where natural processes could replace costly inputs is both ecologically and economically important.

### Amplify It

If natural recovery processes are already underway, these strategies help accelerate and sustain them.

##### Protect what's recovering

Natural recovery is vulnerable to disruption, especially in early stages. Protecting areas of early recovery (no-take zones, seasonal restrictions, exclusion of the most damaging practices) gives natural processes the time and space they need to build momentum. The protection doesn't have to be permanent or total; it has to be sufficient for recovery to take hold.

##### Design interventions that mimic ecosystem structure

The closer a human intervention comes to replicating how a healthy ecosystem organizes itself, the more it can draw on natural processes for its own maintenance. Agroforestry that replicates forest structure gets natural weed suppression, natural pest control, and natural soil fertility as a result: not as an add-on, but as emergent properties of the design. Ask: what would a healthy version of this ecosystem look like, and how close can we get to that structure?

##### Recruit natural allies deliberately

Many restoration interventions work by recruiting natural processes as active partners: bird perches that bring insect-eating birds into fields, trap crops that redirect pest pressure, earthen structures that direct water where it's needed. Identifying the natural processes most useful for the specific restoration, and designing interventions that engage them, multiplies the effect of community effort.

##### Reduce inputs as natural processes strengthen

One of the best signals that nature is doing the work is that human inputs become less necessary over time. As predator populations recover, pest management requires fewer neem applications. As soil fertility rebuilds, chemical fertilizers become unnecessary. As forests restore watershed function, irrigation requirements drop. Track these reductions; they're evidence that the restoration is working, and they're the economic argument that sustains community commitment.

##### Let the ecosystem lead where restoration sequencing is uncertain

In complex ecosystems, it's often impossible to predict exactly how recovery will unfold. Starting with the interventions most likely to trigger natural recovery processes (removing the primary pressure, protecting a core area, restoring a keystone element) and then observing what happens allows the ecosystem's own recovery logic to guide what comes next.

### Build It

If the community hasn't yet identified or engaged natural recovery processes, these strategies help establish the foundation.

##### Map what's still functioning

Even severely degraded ecosystems retain functional elements: soil organisms, seed banks, remnant vegetation, surviving predator populations. Mapping what's still present, with the help of community knowledge and outside expertise, identifies the starting points for natural recovery. You need to know what you have before you can work with it.

##### Start with the intervention most likely to unlock natural recovery

Not all interventions are equal in their capacity to trigger natural processes. A no-take zone that allows fish reproduction to outpace harvest. A dam that restores groundwater and allows vegetation to recover. An end to chemical pesticide use that lets predator populations rebound. Identifying the single intervention most likely to set natural recovery in motion, and starting there, is often more effective than a comprehensive program that tries to address everything at once.

##### Learn from similar ecosystems nearby

If the local ecosystem is too degraded to show clear signs of recovery potential, look to similar ecosystems in the region that are in better condition. What species are present? What processes are operating? What did recovery look like where it has happened? These neighboring ecosystems provide a model for what natural recovery in this context might look like and what it requires.

##### Bring in ecological knowledge to identify what's missing

Sometimes the barrier to natural recovery is a specific missing element (a keystone species, a soil organism, a hydrological connection) whose absence prevents recovery from beginning. Outside ecological expertise can help identify what's missing and whether it can be reintroduced or substituted for. This is one of the highest-value contributions outside partners can make.

### Work Around It

##### When the ecosystem is so degraded that natural recovery processes are no longer functioning

Active restoration may be necessary to reach the threshold at which natural processes can take over: replanting native species, reintroducing key organisms, rebuilding physical structures like water channels or terraces. The goal is to reach the point where nature can take over, not to substitute for natural processes indefinitely. Design active interventions with the question: at what point does this become self-sustaining?

##### When natural recovery is too slow to maintain community commitment

If ecological recovery is visible only over years or decades, community commitment is hard to sustain without intermediate results. Running parallel activities with faster payoffs (home gardens, small enterprises, livelihood diversification) provides the early wins that keep communities engaged while longer-term ecological recovery proceeds. The restoration of natural processes and the maintenance of community commitment are both necessary, and they may require different timelines.

##### When key natural processes have been permanently disrupted

Some ecosystem changes are difficult or impossible to reverse: species extinctions, soil losses, hydrological changes from large-scale infrastructure. Where natural recovery toward the original state isn't possible, the goal shifts: what is the most ecologically functional and community-beneficial state that natural processes can reach from here, given current conditions? Working with nature means accepting what nature can realistically do, not only what it once did. Where a forest can't return, a complex agroforestry system built in its place may be the most functional and productive state the land can reach.

# Ingredient: Mobilizing Community Commitment

## Mobilizing Community Commitment

Powerful symbols inspire commitment and endurance. Quick results in early stages of action strengthen commitment even further.

It is common for charismatic leaders, cherished sites in a local landscape, or other prominent features of a community's success to serve as symbols that inspire commitment and endurance. Quick success, or "payback," in early stages of community action helps to mobilize commitment even further. Once positive results cascade through community and ecosystem, normal social, economic, and political processes reinforce the turnabout.

### Recognize It

The checklist below will help you recognize what sources of symbolic power and early success you already have, so that you can amplify what exists and build on what does not.

- Do you have a site, landmark, or natural feature that holds special meaning for your community?
Cherished places often become powerful symbols of what's worth protecting. These can be historical sites, sacred spaces, gathering places, or natural features that define community identity.

- Does your community have a shared story or narrative about the crisis you're facing?
Having a common understanding of what happened and what's at stake creates emotional investment in solutions. When people share the same story about decline, they're more likely to commit to reversal.

- Are there respected community figures who could champion the cause?
Charismatic or trusted leaders provide a focal point for commitment. Their endorsement gives legitimacy and inspires others to take action.

- Can you identify early wins that would be visible within 1-3 years?
Quick payback helps maintain momentum when the path forward is uncertain. Visible improvements demonstrate that effort is paying off, strengthening resolve to continue.

- Does your planned intervention include measurable benchmarks?
Having clear markers of progress allows you to recognize and celebrate early success, which reinforces commitment at crucial moments.

### Amplify It

If you have elements of symbolic power or potential for rapid results in place, it's important to nurture and amplify them, since they help sustain commitment through difficult periods.

##### Formalize the symbolic importance of key sites or features

If a place already holds meaning, make that status explicit. This might mean declaring it protected, creating ceremonies around it, or incorporating it into community identity in visible ways.

##### Tell the success story repeatedly

As positive results emerge, integrate them into how your community talks about itself. The narrative of "we were in decline, we took action, things improved" becomes a central part of identity that reinforces continued commitment.

##### Make early wins highly visible

When improvements occur, ensure the community sees them. This might mean organizing site visits, creating before/after documentation, or gathering to witness recovery directly.

##### Connect symbolic sites to tangible benefits

The most effective symbols aren't just emotionally meaningful; they deliver concrete improvements to people's lives. When a protected area leads to better catches nearby, or a restored watershed provides cleaner water, the symbol becomes inseparable from practical benefit.

##### Build celebration into your process

Recognize achievements publicly and collectively. This creates positive associations with the hard work of restoration and strengthens the sense that effort leads to reward.

### Build It

If symbolic power or early wins aren't naturally present, there are strategies for creating them deliberately.

##### Choose interventions that can show results quickly

When designing your approach, consider what will deliver visible improvement within a timeframe that maintains commitment, typically 1-3 years. This might mean starting with a smaller protected area that can recover quickly, rather than attempting comprehensive change that takes decades to show results.

##### Create symbols intentionally

If no natural focal point exists, establish one. This might be a pilot site that demonstrates the approach, a monument marking the beginning of restoration, or a regular gathering that becomes associated with your work.

##### Document the journey

From the beginning, capture the state of things before intervention. This creates a baseline against which even modest early improvements become visible and meaningful.

##### Link your work to existing sources of pride

Connect restoration efforts to aspects of community identity that people already value. If the community takes pride in traditional practices, frame restoration as reclaiming those practices. If independence is valued, frame it as local control over local resources.

### Work Around It

Powerful symbols and rapid results are valuable but not always achievable. If neither is available, commitment can still be maintained through other means.

##### Be transparent about timelines

If results will take years, explain why clearly. People can sustain commitment through long processes if they understand what's realistic. Hidden timelines create disappointment; honest ones create realistic expectations.

##### Create intermediate milestones

If the ultimate goal is distant, break it into smaller achievements that can be recognized along the way. These become the wins that maintain momentum even when the final result is years away.

##### Focus on process victories

When outcome victories aren't yet visible, celebrate process achievements. Successfully establishing a management system, achieving broad participation, or overcoming a specific obstacle are all wins that demonstrate progress even before ecological recovery is apparent.

##### Build tangible benefits independent of ecological outcomes

If ecosystem recovery will be slow, ensure other community benefits emerge sooner. This might be improved infrastructure, new income sources, enhanced education, or strengthened social networks. These demonstrate that effort is yielding returns even as ecological restoration continues.

# Ingredient: Overcoming Social Obstacles

Ingredient

## Overcoming Social Obstacles

Every restoration faces resistance. The communities that succeed find ways through it, not by avoiding conflict, but by meeting it with strategy, solidarity, and persistence.

##### Ingredients for Success Tool

↓ Download as PDF

A printable version of this tool to take into the field.

##### Related Ingredients

→ Shared community awareness and commitment
→ Enduring commitment of local leadership
→ Outside stimulation and facilitation

Social obstacles are not exceptions to the restoration process; they are part of it. Communities attempting to reverse environmental decline will encounter resistance from within: people who fear change, who can't afford the risk of trying something new, or whose short-term interests conflict with the collective good. They will encounter resistance from outside: commercial interests threatened by community autonomy, government agencies that don't recognize local authority, powerful actors who benefit from the status quo. The communities that succeed don't avoid these conflicts. They anticipate them, develop strategies for navigating them, and build the collective strength to persist through them.

### Recognize It

- Is there internal resistance (community members who are skeptical, fearful, or actively opposed to the proposed change)?
Internal resistance is almost universal in early-stage restoration. Some community members will have more to lose from change than others, or will have been burned by failed initiatives before, or simply won't believe the proposed solution can work. Recognizing this as normal (and understanding its specific sources) is the starting point for addressing it.

- Are there community members whose economic situation makes the risk of trying something new genuinely prohibitive?
Skepticism rooted in poverty or debt is different from skepticism rooted in preference or habit. Families who are one failed crop away from starvation, or who are locked into debt relationships with suppliers or landlords, face real constraints that abstract arguments about long-term benefit won't overcome. These constraints need to be addressed directly, not argued past.

- Does the proposed restoration put the community in conflict with outside authority (government agencies, national laws, commercial operators, neighboring communities)?
Many restorations eventually require communities to assert control over resources that outside actors also claim: fisheries, forests, water, land. This is predictable, and communities are better served by anticipating it and developing a strategy than by hoping it won't arise.

- Are there actors outside the community who benefit from the status quo and have the means to actively obstruct change?
Pesticide dealers, commercial fishing operations, mining companies, landlords: actors whose income depends on the community's continued dependence have both the motive and sometimes the means to push back hard. Naming these actors and their interests honestly is essential for developing effective responses.

- Has the community found ways to design its rules and governance so that they're enforceable without requiring heroic effort?
Rules that depend on constant vigilance, high enforcement costs, or the goodwill of people with conflicting interests tend to fail. The most durable rules are those that are easy to monitor, understood and accepted by most community members, and carry consequences that are consistently applied.

### Amplify It

If the community is already navigating social obstacles, these strategies strengthen its capacity to persist.

##### Make the rules easy to enforce

The most effective governance solutions are ones that minimize the cost of enforcement. Apo Island's no-fishing sanctuary rule could be monitored by a single person watching from the beach: a task rotated among families so no one bore it alone. When enforcement is simple and shared, it's sustainable. When it requires constant effort from a dedicated few, it eventually breaks down.

##### Build collective responses to economic pressure

When outside actors use economic leverage to punish communities for changing (paying less for goods, calling in debts, withdrawing credit) individual community members are vulnerable. Collective responses change the equation: marketing cooperatives find alternative buyers, mutual aid networks cover gaps, unified negotiating positions force creditors to accept reasonable terms. The Punukula farmers who faced pesticide dealer retaliation didn't resist individually; they organized collectively, and won.

##### Convert skeptics through results, not argument

The most effective response to internal skepticism is visible success. Early adopters who demonstrate that the new approach works (that yields improve, that costs fall, that fish return) provide evidence that no amount of advocacy can match. Design for early wins that skeptics can see and verify, and let results do the persuading.

##### Give a potential spoiler a stake in success

Sometimes the most effective way to neutralize opposition is to bring the opponent inside. In a Thai project, a district official who was involved in illegal logging in the project communities was given a seat on the project's board of trustees. The discomfort of sitting in restoration meetings among those communities led him to stop the logging.

##### Establish legal and government relationships before you need them

Communities that wait until a conflict with outside authority has reached a crisis before seeking legal standing or government recognition are at a disadvantage. Building those relationships earlier (when there's less pressure) creates the foundation for effective advocacy when disputes arise. Knowing which government bodies have relevant authority, and which officials are potential allies, is preparation that pays off.

##### Use nonviolent collective action when other avenues are blocked

When legal channels fail and negotiation stalls, nonviolent collective action (public demonstrations, organized civil disobedience, strategic use of media) has been effective in cases where communities faced powerful outside opposition. TBS's Water Warriors combined legal action with nonviolent resistance when the government tried to stop the johad movement and when mining companies responded to legal challenges with physical intimidation. Having a range of tools, and being willing to use them, matters.

### Build It

If the community hasn't yet developed strategies for navigating social obstacles, these approaches help establish them.

##### Name the obstacles honestly before they arrive

The most useful preparation for social obstacles is to anticipate them specifically, not generically. Who in the community is most likely to resist, and why? Which outside actors have interests that conflict with restoration? What legal or regulatory barriers exist? Naming these concretely, before they become active conflicts, allows the community to prepare rather than react.

##### Start with changes that require the least social disruption

Gradual conversion (starting with willing early adopters, demonstrating results, and expanding as evidence accumulates) reduces the risk that resistant community members need to take on before they've seen proof that the approach works. Khao Din converted to agroforestry gradually precisely because the financial stakes were too high for families to risk everything at once. The sequencing of change matters.

##### Design for low-cost cooperation

Rules and collective actions that make reasonable demands on people's time and energy are more likely to be sustained than those that require exceptional effort. Ask: what's the simplest governance arrangement that would work? What's the minimum viable collective commitment that gets things started? Start there, and build complexity only as trust and capacity develop.

##### Develop outside relationships that can provide legal, financial, or political support

Communities facing outside opposition rarely succeed alone. Outside organizations (legal aid groups, advocacy NGOs, government allies, university partners) can provide resources, expertise, and credibility that shift the balance when communities face powerful opposition. Building these relationships before they're urgently needed is far easier than trying to establish them under pressure.

### Work Around It

##### When internal opposition is strong enough to block collective action

Start smaller. Work with the subset of community members who are willing, demonstrate results at a scale that doesn't require buy-in from opponents, and let evidence accumulate. Forced consensus that papers over genuine opposition tends to collapse under pressure; voluntary adoption that spreads as results become visible tends to hold.

##### When outside opposition is too powerful to confront directly

Find the paths of least resistance. Legal channels, government allies, outside advocacy organizations, and public attention can all shift the power balance in situations where direct confrontation would fail. TBS didn't win every battle through force of will; it built a movement large enough, and with enough legal and public legitimacy, that even powerful opponents couldn't simply ignore it.

##### When the economic constraints facing community members are too severe for gradual conversion

Address economic vulnerability directly before asking people to take on additional risk. This might mean working with outside organizations to provide bridge support, designing interventions that use labor rather than cash, or identifying quick livelihood wins that reduce financial pressure enough to make experimentation possible. A community that is financially desperate cannot absorb the risk of change, however promising.

##### When government or legal authority actively blocks restoration

Document everything, build outside alliances, and look for legal or political leverage points. Communities that have successfully challenged government opposition have typically done so by combining legal action with public visibility: making the cost of continued opposition higher than the cost of accommodation. This takes time and outside support, but it has worked in situations that initially seemed unwinnable.

# Ingredient: Social and Ecological Diversity

Ingredient

## Social and Ecological Diversity

Diversity is insurance. Communities and ecosystems with more variety have more options, more buffers, and more paths forward when conditions change.

##### Ingredients for Success Tool

↓ Download as PDF

A printable version of this tool to take into the field.

##### Related Ingredients

→ Letting nature do the work
→ Building resilience
→ Social and ecological memory

Monocultures (ecological or social) are fragile. A single crop fails in a bad season. A single income source collapses when markets shift. A single decision-making voice misses what others would have caught. Diversity, by contrast, provides options: if one crop fails, another succeeds; if one livelihood is disrupted, another absorbs the shock; if one perspective is wrong, another corrects it. In restoration, social and ecological diversity reinforce each other. A more diverse ecosystem provides more livelihood options. More diverse livelihoods reduce the pressure on any single ecosystem resource. And a community with diverse knowledge, skills, and connections is better equipped to manage a complex ecosystem over time.

### Recognize It

- Does the community's farming, fishing, or resource use draw on multiple species, methods, or income sources?
Diversification that already exists (even informally, even at small scale) is a foundation to build on. Home gardens alongside field crops, multiple fish species targeted by different methods, cottage industries that process farm and forest products: each of these represents an existing buffer against the failure of any single source.

- Does the local ecosystem support a variety of species and ecological relationships?
Ecological diversity is both a goal and a mechanism. Diverse ecosystems are more stable, more self-regulating, and more resilient to disruption than simplified ones. Signs of existing diversity (multiple plant species, visible predator populations, varied habitat types) indicate that the ecosystem retains functional complexity worth protecting and building on.

- Does community decision-making draw on a range of voices and perspectives?
Social diversity in governance (women alongside men, younger alongside older, different livelihood groups represented) tends to produce better decisions and broader buy-in than governance that reflects only a narrow set of perspectives. Ask: whose knowledge and whose interests are currently included in how the community makes decisions, and whose are missing?

- Has the community developed connections beyond its own boundaries: to other communities, to outside organizations, to markets or institutions?
Social diversity isn't only internal. Communities that have developed connections to the wider world (through outside partnerships, market relationships, peer networks, or institutional ties) have access to a broader range of options and resources when local conditions change. Apo Island's dialogue with Silliman University scientists diversified the community's awareness of what was possible before any physical restoration began.

- Are livelihoods distributed across enough different activities that the failure of one doesn't threaten the whole community?
The test of livelihood diversity is resilience under stress: if the fishery has a bad year, do families have other income? If crop prices fall, does the community have other ways to meet its needs? Diversification that exists only on paper (or that everyone engages in identically) provides less protection than genuine variety across households and activities.

### Amplify It

If diversity is already present in the community or ecosystem, these strategies deepen and extend it.

##### Expand livelihood diversity deliberately alongside ecological restoration

As restoration proceeds and ecosystem health improves, new livelihood opportunities often emerge: tourism, new products, processing enterprises, training and hosting roles for other communities. Planning for these opportunities rather than letting them develop accidentally ensures that the economic benefits of restoration are distributed broadly and that the community's livelihood base grows alongside its ecological base.

##### Protect and expand the variety of species and habitats in the ecosystem

Ecological diversity is self-reinforcing up to a point (more species support more species), but it requires protection to get there. Ensuring that restoration doesn't create new monocultures (even ecologically beneficial ones) and that habitat variety is maintained or expanded gives the ecosystem more pathways for recovery and more resistance to future disruption.

##### Actively include underrepresented voices in governance

Diversity in decision-making doesn't happen automatically; it requires deliberate inclusion of people whose perspectives are typically absent or marginalized. Women's groups, youth, community members from different livelihood backgrounds, people with experience in other places: each brings knowledge and interests that improve the quality of collective decisions. Building structures that make this inclusion normal rather than exceptional is the goal.

##### Use outside connections to expand the community's awareness of options

One of the highest-value forms of social diversity is exposure to how other communities have addressed similar problems: what approaches exist, what has worked elsewhere, what's possible that the community doesn't yet know about. Outside partnerships, peer exchanges, and visits to other sites all diversify the community's sense of what's available to it.

##### Build redundancy into critical systems

Diversity as insurance means having backup options for the things that matter most. Multiple water sources in case one fails. Multiple governance mechanisms in case one is captured or undermined. Multiple market channels in case one closes. Deliberate redundancy (especially in systems the community depends on most) is a practical expression of the diversity principle.

### Build It

If diversity is limited (ecologically, economically, or socially), these strategies help establish it.

##### Start diversification where risk is lowest

Asking families to diversify livelihoods when they're financially precarious asks them to take on risk they may not be able to absorb. Starting with diversification that requires minimal upfront investment: home gardens, small-scale processing of existing products, cottage industries that use available materials, builds a foundation without requiring a bet the community can't afford to lose.

##### Use the restoration itself as a platform for livelihood diversification

Restoration activities often create livelihood opportunities directly: hosting visitors, training other communities, producing and selling seedlings or compost, managing ecotourism. Building these opportunities into the restoration design from the beginning means that economic diversity grows alongside ecological recovery, not as an afterthought.

##### Introduce ecological diversity through the choice of restoration species and methods

Where degradation has simplified the ecosystem, restoration design can rebuild variety deliberately: by choosing polyculture over monoculture, by reintroducing multiple native species rather than a single dominant one, by restoring varied habitat types rather than a single preferred condition. Each additional species or habitat type is an additional ecological function and an additional option for the future.

##### Develop the community's connections to outside networks and knowledge

Social diversity is partly internal (more voices, more perspectives) and partly relational: more connections to the world beyond the community. Facilitating these connections to peer communities, to outside organizations, to markets and institutions that can provide options the community doesn't currently have access to, builds the external dimension of social diversity.

### Work Around It

##### When the community's livelihoods are locked into a single system by debt or dependency

Diversification requires some degree of freedom to experiment, which debt and dependency can eliminate. Where families are locked into purchasing and selling relationships that constrain their options (through debt to input suppliers, through contract farming arrangements, through dependence on a single buyer) addressing those dependencies directly may be a prerequisite for meaningful diversification. Collective action, as Punukula demonstrated, can sometimes break these dependencies where individual action cannot.

##### When the ecosystem has been so simplified that ecological diversity is very low

Severely degraded ecosystems may need active intervention to reintroduce diversity: replanting multiple native species, managing for habitat variety, reintroducing species that have been locally eliminated. This is slower and more resource-intensive than working with existing diversity, but it creates the ecological foundation that natural recovery processes need to build on.

##### When governance consistently excludes key voices

If women, young people, or specific livelihood groups are systematically excluded from community decision-making, formal inclusion mechanisms alone rarely fix the problem. Building parallel spaces where underrepresented groups can develop their own collective voice: women's cooperatives, youth groups, livelihood associations, creates constituencies that eventually change who gets heard in broader governance.

# Ingredient: Social and Ecological Memory

Ingredient

## Social and Ecological Memory

The past holds blueprints. Communities and ecosystems that can draw on what worked before have a head start on what to do next.

##### Ingredients for Success Tool

↓ Download as PDF

A printable version of this tool to take into the field.

##### Related Ingredients

→ Harmony between community and ecosystem
→ Letting nature do the work
→ Outside stimulation and facilitation

Every community and every ecosystem carries memory: knowledge, practices, and biological designs that have been tested and refined over generations. Social memory lives in elders who remember what the land looked like before decline, in traditional practices that governed how resources were used, in stories and values that shaped how people related to their environment. Ecological memory lives in nature itself: in the evolutionary design of organisms and ecosystems that, given the chance, know how to recover and rebuild. When restoration draws on both kinds of memory together, communities aren't starting from scratch. They're recovering something that already worked.

### Recognize It

- Do community elders or long-term residents remember what the local environment looked like before degradation began?
This memory (of fuller rivers, richer soils, more abundant fish, denser forests) provides a reference point for what restoration might achieve. It also tells you where the ecosystem's potential still exists, even if it's not visible now.

- Does the community have traditional practices for managing shared natural resources that were in use before the decline?
Traditional fishing methods, agricultural techniques, water management structures, forest use customs: these practices represent generations of local adaptation. Even if they've been abandoned or disrupted, they often remain in living memory.

- Are there traditional values or principles governing how people relate to nature that could support restoration?
Values like "take only what you need," seasonal restrictions on harvesting, or the idea that the land belongs to future generations as much as to the present: these aren't just cultural artifacts. They are functional rules for sustainable resource use.

- Does the ecosystem itself show signs it retains the capacity to recover?
Ecological memory is nature's built-in recovery capacity: the seeds still in the soil, the predator species that return once conditions improve, the coral polyps that recolonize a reef given protection. Evidence that recovery has happened elsewhere, or in small pockets locally, is evidence that ecological memory is intact.

- Is there knowledge of where traditional resource management worked well, or where ecosystem health was strongest in the past?
Knowing where to focus restoration (which reef had the richest coral, which forest had the best watershed function, which fields were most productive) can guide decisions about where to begin.

### Amplify It

If social and ecological memory are already present, here are strategies for putting them to work.

##### Surface and document what people remember

Create opportunities for elders and long-term residents to share what they know: through community meetings, structured interviews, participatory mapping of how the landscape looked in the past. This knowledge is often undervalued until it's explicitly recognized as useful. Making it visible strengthens its legitimacy as a guide for action.

##### Connect traditional practices to modern approaches

Traditional practices and modern science often reinforce each other. Agroforestry that mimics the structure of a natural forest draws on both. Natural pest management draws on traditional knowledge of plants and predators, backed by ecological science. Looking for these connections avoids the false choice between "traditional" and "modern" and makes both more effective.

##### Let the ecosystem lead where it can

Ecological memory means that ecosystems, given the right conditions, know how to recover. Where the memory is intact (where soils, seeds, and species still hold the blueprint), the community's job is often to remove the pressure and let recovery begin, rather than to engineer it. This is also the basis of the related ingredient, Letting Nature Do the Work.

##### Use memory to set realistic and motivating goals

Knowing that the reef was once rich with fish, or that the river once ran year-round, makes restoration concrete rather than abstract. These memories translate into goals the community can recognize and measure: goals that are ambitious enough to matter but grounded in what this specific ecosystem has actually done before.

### Build It

If social and ecological memory have been lost or are severely depleted, these strategies can help reconstruct them.

##### Seek out anyone who remembers

Even in communities with significant population turnover or cultural disruption, there are often individuals (elders, returned migrants, people from neighboring communities) who carry fragments of useful memory. Seek them out deliberately. A single person's recollection of traditional water management practices or fishing customs can be enough to begin.

##### Look to neighboring communities or ecosystems

If local memory has been lost, adjacent communities or similar ecosystems may preserve it. Communities that maintained traditional practices can serve as models. Ecosystems nearby that were less degraded can show what recovery looks like and what species should return.

##### Use ecological evidence to reconstruct the past

Old photographs, land records, oral histories from outside the community, scientific surveys from earlier decades: these can help reconstruct what the ecosystem once looked like and what it might become. Even fragmentary evidence helps set direction.

##### Bring in outside knowledge that connects to local conditions

When internal memory is insufficient, outside experts (scientists, extension workers, practitioners from similar contexts) can help fill the gap. The key is that outside knowledge should be introduced in ways that complement and build on whatever local knowledge remains, not replace it.

### Work Around It

##### When elders have died or left, and traditional knowledge has been lost

Start with ecological evidence and neighboring community practices. Be honest with the community that you're working toward a possible future rather than recovering a known past: but make clear that ecosystems in similar conditions have recovered, and that nature's own memory (its capacity for self-restoration) is still working even when human memory is depleted.

##### When traditional practices were themselves unsustainable, or contributed to the decline

Not all traditional practices are worth restoring. Where they were part of the problem, the task is to identify which values and principles remain useful (sustainable harvest levels, collective stewardship, respect for natural cycles) while leaving behind the specific practices that caused harm. Outside knowledge may be more important here than in cases where tradition was genuinely sustainable.

##### When the ecosystem is so degraded that ecological memory is uncertain

Even severely degraded ecosystems often retain more recovery capacity than they appear to. Start small: protect a small area, reduce the key pressure, and watch for signs of recovery. If native species begin to return, if water begins to flow, if soil begins to rebuild, ecological memory is likely intact. Let early results guide how far and how fast to proceed.

# Ingredient: Building Resilience

Ingredient

## Building Resilience

Success doesn't protect itself. Communities that build resilience into their restoration are prepared for the disruptions that will inevitably come.

##### Ingredients for Success Tool

↓ Download as PDF

A printable version of this tool to take into the field.

##### Related Ingredients

→ Harmony between community and ecosystem
→ Social and ecological memory
→ Letting nature do the work

Every restoration faces disruption. Weather events, market shocks, political pressure, population change, the unexpected consequences of success itself: these aren't exceptional circumstances, they're the normal conditions in which restored ecosystems and communities have to survive. Resilience is the capacity to absorb those disruptions, reorganize around them, and keep adapting over time. It doesn't emerge automatically from success; it has to be built deliberately. Communities that build resilience alongside their restoration (strengthening governance, diversifying livelihoods, restoring ecological buffers, developing their own capacity to learn and adjust) protect what they've worked to create.

### Recognize It

- Has the community's restoration increased its ability to absorb shocks (economic, ecological, or social)?
Resilience begins with reduced vulnerability. When a community is less dependent on a single crop, a single income source, or a single governing institution, it has more capacity to absorb disruption without losing what it has built. Look for signs that the restoration itself has created buffers: healthier soils that survive drought better, diversified income that absorbs market shifts, stronger social networks that can mobilize quickly when problems arise.

- Are the community's governance institutions strong enough to adapt when conditions change?
Rules and institutions that worked when a restoration was new may need to evolve as the ecosystem recovers, as new pressures emerge, or as the community's composition changes. Resilient governance has the capacity to revisit, renegotiate, and revise; not just enforce what was originally decided.

- Does the community have mechanisms for learning from experience and adjusting its approach?
Regular reflection on what's working and what isn't (community meetings, seasonal reviews, feedback loops between ecosystem monitoring and management decisions) builds the adaptive capacity that makes resilience possible over time. The capacity to learn is as important as the capacity to absorb shocks.

- Has the restored ecosystem developed ecological buffers that protect against disruption?
Healthy forests reduce erosion and regulate water flow. Diverse agricultural systems buffer against weather variation and pest outbreaks. Recovered fish stocks provide a margin that allows sustainable harvesting even in difficult years. Ecological resilience and community resilience reinforce each other: the healthier the ecosystem, the more buffer it provides; the more resilient the community, the better it can protect and sustain the ecosystem.

- Has the community anticipated the potential complications of its own success?
Success creates new pressures: outside interest, tourism, migration, political attention, resource exploitation by people outside the community. Apo Island found that its recovered fishery attracted poachers and that tourism growth threatened the reef it had worked to protect. Anticipating these second-order effects (before they arrive) is one of the most important forms of resilience planning.

### Amplify It

If resilience-building is already underway, these strategies strengthen it.

##### Diversify livelihoods alongside ecological restoration

Economic resilience requires that families not be entirely dependent on the restored ecosystem for their income. Home gardens, cottage industries, new enterprises, and off-farm income sources provide the financial buffer that makes it possible to manage the ecosystem sustainably rather than exploit it when times are hard. Plan for early wins in this area: visible economic gains in the first year build confidence and buy time for longer-term ecological recovery.

##### Strengthen governance before you need it

Governance institutions are most resilient when they're strengthened during stable periods, not stress-tested for the first time during a crisis. Regularly revisiting community rules, building clear processes for dispute resolution, and developing the next generation of local leadership all build institutional resilience that pays off when disruption arrives.

##### Monitor and respond: build in the feedback loops

Sustained restoration requires regular assessment of how the ecosystem is performing and how community conditions are changing. This doesn't require formal scientific monitoring; community members tracking fish catches, water levels, crop yields, or forest cover can generate the information needed to recognize when something is shifting and respond before a problem becomes a crisis.

##### Build relationships with outside allies before you need them

Outside organizations, government agencies, and legal authorities are most useful when the relationship already exists. Communities that have established connections to legal support, scientific expertise, or government backing can draw on those connections when threats arise, rather than trying to build them under pressure.

##### Plan explicitly for the complications of success

As restoration proceeds, take time to ask: what new pressures will this success attract? Who else might want access to a recovered resource? What outside interests will the community's success make visible? Building governance responses to these questions in advance is far more effective than reacting to them after they've become conflicts.

### Build It

If resilience has not yet been built into the restoration, these strategies help establish it.

##### Start sustainability planning from the beginning, not after success

Resilience is much harder to build after the fact. The governance institutions, livelihood diversification, and ecological buffers that protect a restoration are most effectively developed as the restoration proceeds, not added on once success has been achieved. Even in early phases, it's worth asking: what would threaten what we're building, and what would protect it?

##### Identify the most likely disruptions and prepare for them specifically

Generic resilience is less useful than targeted preparation. What are the specific weather events, market shifts, political pressures, or social changes most likely to threaten this restoration? What would the community need to have in place to absorb each of them? Naming specific risks makes preparation concrete.

##### Use early wins to fund resilience-building

The economic gains from early restoration success (reduced input costs, improved harvests, new income sources) can be channeled deliberately into resilience-building activities: savings systems, community funds, infrastructure investments, or governance development. This connects short-term wins to long-term durability.

##### Develop community capacity for adaptive management

Adaptive management means treating the restoration as an ongoing experiment: acting on the best available knowledge, monitoring results, and adjusting based on what you learn. Building this capacity requires establishing the habits and institutions for regular reflection: community meetings with a genuine review function, willingness to revise decisions, and a culture that treats course correction as a sign of strength rather than failure.

### Work Around It

##### When ecological resilience has been severely depleted

Some ecosystems have lost so much of their buffering capacity (soils stripped bare, watersheds degraded, fish stocks collapsed) that recovery is slow and vulnerable to setbacks. In these situations, focus first on the ecological interventions that rebuild buffer capacity most quickly: watershed protection, soil restoration, removal of the most acute pressures. Expect setbacks and plan for them rather than treating them as failures.

##### When governance institutions are weak or contested

Where community governance is fragile, start small: with rules that are genuinely enforceable and consequences that are actually followed through. Governance resilience is built through consistent, fair enforcement of modest commitments, not through ambitious rules that can't be maintained. Outside facilitation can help, but it can't substitute for community ownership of the governance process.

##### When economic pressure makes sustainable management difficult

When families are under severe financial pressure, they may have no choice but to exploit a recovered resource unsustainably. Resilience planning in these contexts has to address economic vulnerability directly: through livelihood diversification, safety net mechanisms, or targeted support during periods of stress. Ecological and economic resilience are inseparable.

Community Sessions

Structured facilitation guides for NGO practitioners running community workshops that turn systems understanding into collective action.

The systems thinking tool helps a community understand their situation. These sessions are where they do something about it.

Map It and Reverse It are structured facilitation guides for NGO practitioners running community workshops. Slide decks for each session are available on the Map It and Reverse It pages; review them before you run each session, not for the first time in the room with the community members.

Each session comes with supporting resources: a slide deck that visually explains the mapping process and helps you build your own, an Impact Worksheet for evaluating potential levers, and a Feasibility Worksheet for assessing what it would take to carry out a candidate action.

# Community Sessions: Map It

Community Sessions

## Map It

A structured facilitation guide for producing a shared diagram of how a community's ecological and social problems connect and reinforce each other.

##### Community Sessions Tool

↓ Download as PDF

A printable version of this tool to take into the field.

A community mapping session produces a shared diagram showing how the community's ecological and social problems connect and reinforce each other, and where a change might find traction. Collective knowledge is essential, but building it together in the room creates shared understanding and anchors commitment to whatever comes next. People who helped draw the map will engage with it differently than people who were handed one.

### 1. Agree on the problem

Before gathering losses, get the group to a shared starting point. This takes ten minutes and prevents the session from fracturing into separate threads later.

Ask: "In one sentence, what is the main problem this community is facing?" Write responses. If they diverge, find the common thread and name it. Write it at the top of the paper as the agreed framing for the session.

### 2. Open with loss, not process

Don't explain the exercise upfront. Start with a concrete question about lived experience:

> "Think back 20 or 30 years. What was different here: in the forest, the water, the fishing, the farming, the animals, the soil? What do you remember that you don't see anymore? What has become harder (in the fields, the home, the community) that used to be easier?"

Write responses on the paper as they come. Don't organize yet. Invite different perspectives: what fishing families lost may differ from what farming households lost; what elders remember may differ from what younger people have seen. Make sure all of it goes on the paper: ecological losses and social ones together.

If the group is quiet, try: "What did your parents have access to that you don't?"

Then push back in time further. Ask about changes that happened before the visible decline, in the decades leading up to it: new land management practices, shifts in ownership, industries or markets that arrived and changed how people used the land or water. External pressures (government policies, new technologies, outside economic forces) belong on this list as much as local ones. Some of the earliest shifts are where the story actually starts.

### 3. Surface the pressures

Once you have a list of losses, ask about causes. No cause is too large or too small:

> "Why is there less [fish / forest / water / healthy soil] than there used to be? What changed?"

Keep collecting on the same paper without organizing. People typically name external causes first: new industries, government land decisions, outside markets. Acknowledge these as real. Then ask: "And within the community, is there anything happening here that's also contributing?"

### 4. Build the map

Pick one item, usually a core resource loss, and draw it in the center. Then ask:

> "What caused this to happen? What happened first, before this?"

Draw the earliest cause they identify. Then: "What happened next?" Draw the arrow forward. Repeat this, working chronologically toward the present. The diagram grows as the story proceeds, and that's intentional: participants should see the chain extending as the session goes on.

Arrows can lead in multiple directions from the same event. They can also loop back to something already in the chain, which is when the diagram starts showing why things keep getting worse.

No impact is too small to map. Ecological changes spread across both the ecosystem and the social system. When participants mention something that seems minor, add it.

##### Facilitator Tip

When a vicious cycle first becomes visible in the diagram, stop and name it explicitly. "So this loops back to here, which means the problem feeds itself. That's the pattern we're looking for." These reinforcing loops are often the most important places to break.

The session is working when someone in the room says "so it keeps getting worse because…" That's the feedback loop becoming visible.

If the group gets stuck: "Does this connect to anything else on the board?" or "Is there anything important that's missing from this picture?"

Once the diagram feels complete, ask participants to identify the earliest cause in the chain: the event or change that started the whole sequence. Circle it. This is the negative tipping point: the moment the system began moving in the wrong direction. Label it. You'll return to it in the next session.

##### Facilitator Tip

Three things are distinct on this diagram: the negative tipping point (where the system began to tip); the specific location in the chain where the human-nature connection broke; and the nodes where a positive intervention might work. They often overlap, but not always. Knowing which is which matters for Reverse It.

### 5. Test and validate

Before closing, make sure the complete diagram is on the paper. Then read it back:

> "So what we're seeing is: [walk through the loop]. Does this capture what's happening here? What's wrong with this picture? Is this leaving out any very important losses or potential causes?"

Give people explicit permission to correct it. Then ask: "Is there anything on this map that the community has some ability to change, even partially?" Circle those nodes lightly. This is a positive note to end on: the group has found places where they might have some traction.

Close with a brief, open question: "Is there anything in this diagram that we could change?" Don't take it further here. Just let the answers sit. That's the question Reverse It is built to answer.

##### Facilitator Note

This question appears again at the start of Reverse It. That's intentional; it's a bridge, not a repetition. Ending Map It with it open plants the question before the group has a framework to answer it. Reverse It gives them that framework.

### 6. Close and distribute

Before people leave, name what the group accomplished: they've described their situation as a connected system rather than a list of separate problems, and that's what points toward where a change could matter.

The map should leave the room with participants. A shared diagram they can hold, study, and show to others does work that a map on the facilitator's laptop cannot. Photograph the original at the end of the session. If time allows, redraw a clean version before distributing, especially if the map got complicated. A clear layout, with vicious cycles and the tipping point visually legible, helps people keep thinking between sessions.

### Watch For

Deference to one or two voices. Try breaking into smaller groups for part of the discussion. Ask quieter participants directly: "What do you see from your experience?"

Politeness masking disagreement. In many communities, people will nod or raise hands to be polite without intending to act. Don't read silence as agreement. Pause, ask the quiet people, and when someone nods, ask what the nod means.

Impatience for solutions. Acknowledge the urgency: "I know it feels slow to spend time mapping when things are urgent. What we're trying to avoid is acting fast in the wrong place."

A map that gets too complicated. Pull back: "If you had to identify just three or four things driving the decline, what would they be?" Some maps do get complex; that's not always a problem. But participants should be able to read it.

### Materials Note

Large paper is ideal, but work with what's available: a whiteboard, a wall with sticky notes, or even a cleared patch of ground and sticks.

Two marker colors help distinguish problems from connections, but one color works.

Photograph the map at the end of the session and print before the next one. Printed copies for distribution matter. A shared map that participants can hold and show to others does work that a map on the facilitator's laptop cannot.

# Community Sessions: Reverse It

Community Sessions

## Reverse It

A structured facilitation guide for using the community map to find where and how to intervene, working toward a shortlist of candidate interventions specific enough to plan a first step.

##### Community Sessions Tool

↓ Download as PDF

A printable version of this tool to take into the field.

This session uses the map the community built to find where and how to intervene. There's no formula: every community faces a different set of environmental and social challenges. But this framework will guide the community in their search for strategies that are forceful and doable: a vicious cycle mapped, levers strong enough to turn things around identified, and a test of whether the community has the resources and commitment to pull it off.

You're working toward a shortlist of 1–3 candidate interventions specific enough to plan a first step.

### 1. Reconnect to the map

Distribute printed copies of the Map It diagram. Having the map in hand changes the dynamic: participants can annotate, point, and return to it throughout the session.

Walk through it briefly. Don't assume people remember the previous session:

> "Last time we mapped how [X, Y, Z] connect and reinforce each other. At the end, the group identified places where the community might have some ability to act. Today: what would actually change this?"

Return to those circled nodes from the Map It session. Invite participants to add any new ones they've thought about since. That's where this session begins.

### 2. Look for where the connection broke

Before the decline started, the ecosystem and community usually supported each other. Something broke that relationship: a new market, a technology, outside pressure, a policy change. Those break points are worth locating precisely.

Ask:

> "Where in this diagram did things once work differently? Was there a time when the health of [the forest / the fish / the soil] and the wellbeing of families here supported each other, before they started working against each other?"

Mark those points. There may be several; in a system that has spiralled deeply, an early break often gets amplified by reinforcing cycles that came later. Look for breaks at different points in the chain, not just the earliest one.

Then ask which of them the community can actually influence. The overlap between "where the connection broke" and "where we have agency" is the shortlist.

This overlap is a useful starting point for finding levers. Some levers will map directly to where the break happened. Others will work at different points in the chain. The goal is to find the places with the most pull on the whole system.

### 3. Ask if someone nearby already solved this

Before designing anything, ask:

> "Is there a household, a village, a farm nearby where this problem doesn't exist, or where it used to exist and got better? What are they doing differently?"

If yes, study it before inventing anything. A solution developed within the same ecological and social constraints (same soils, same seasons, same pressures) is often more credible and easier to adopt than one brought in from elsewhere.

### 4. Introduce patterns from similar cases

Bring in ETP case material as analogy, not prescription. For each candidate intervention point, ask whether any ETP case looks structurally similar:

- Resource extracted faster than it regenerates (including reef and fish populations): Apo Island. Fishers agreed on two rules: a permanent no-take sanctuary covering a fifth of the reef, and a ban on destructive fishing methods. Fish populations recovered within a few years, and the surrounding fishery improved because the protected area was producing more fish than before. The community gave the reef the conditions to restore itself.
- Community benefit disconnected from ecological health: Khao Din. Restructure farming so the land's health and the family's income move together.
- Dependence on external inputs: Punukula. Replace cash inputs with labor and local knowledge.
- Degraded water or soil: Gopalpura. Restore the infrastructure that natural cycles depend on to support community life.
The question isn't "do what they did." It's: what structural move made this work, and is that move available here?

### 5. Generate lever candidates

Ask the group to identify 3–5 specific things the community could do that might shift the system. Push past the obvious: the most effective lever often isn't the most visible one. Encourage candidates that work with what the ecosystem can do on its own, given the right conditions.

Useful prompts:

> "If you could change one thing in this diagram that you actually have some control over, what would it be?"

> "What would have to be true for the ecosystem to begin recovering on its own?"

> "Is there a practice, a rule, or a resource that used to exist here that the community has the knowledge to restore?"

A lever that draws on ecological memory (on species behaviors and ecosystem cycles the community already understands) is often more durable than one that requires new outside knowledge. Nature's own self-healing capacity is one of the most powerful forces available; the facilitator's job is to help the group find where to let it through.

##### Facilitator Tip

If you already have a sense of what the lever should be, hold it back. Share it only after the group has generated its own candidates. Present it as one more option to evaluate. A process that performs participation while seeking ratification is the most common failure mode in this work.

### 6. Test each candidate

Run 3–5 candidates through a group feasibility check. The questions below apply to rules, collective agreements, restoration practices, governance changes, and other types of initiatives:

- Can people tell whether this is being followed or carried out?
- Can the community track progress without sustained outside help?
- Can it be adapted as conditions change, using knowledge that already exists here?
- Does it require governance structures that don't yet exist?
- Can the community provide the time, energy, and resources to carry it out?
- Will it involve short-term sacrifices that create excessive burdens for some members? If so, how will those burdens be shared?
- Does the community have the leadership needed to manage and enforce commitments?
- Starting small: can you run a pilot version that builds commitment and resources before scaling up?
- Does this change who benefits from the resource, or does it just change how things are done while leaving the same people in control?
### 7. Rank and close

Help the group rank candidates on three dimensions: how many loops in the system does this affect (leverage), how significantly does it shift the system dynamic (impact), and what can they realistically start (feasibility). Give weight to candidates that harness natural recovery processes (what the land or water will do if given the chance).

Close with:

> "The point isn't to find the perfect lever. It's to find one worth testing. What would tell you, within the first year or two, whether this is working?"

Use the Impact Worksheet to quantify how much leverage and impact each candidate actually has before ranking them. ↓ Download the Impact Worksheet

### Watch For

The group jumps to solutions early. Let them name the solution, then run it through the feasibility check rather than shutting down the energy.

Every option feels too small. Usually means the group has correctly identified that real change requires structural shifts that feel politically impossible. Ask: "Given that, what's a move that still points in that direction?"

The group can't agree. Either the analysis wasn't complete (go back to the map) or there's a real conflict of interest that hasn't surfaced yet. Ask: "What's the disagreement actually about?"

You already know what the lever should be. Share it only after the group has generated its own candidates, and present it as one more option to evaluate. Running a process that performs participation while actually seeking ratification is the most common failure mode in this work.

### Materials Note

Bring printed copies of the Map It diagram and distribute them at the start of the session. A map participants can hold and mark up changes the dynamic.

# Case Study: Apo Island, Philippines

Case Study

## Apo Island, Philippines

When Stopping Fishing Saved the Fishery

By the late 1970s, fishermen on Apo Island were traveling 10 kilometers from home to catch enough fish to feed their families. The coral reefs surrounding their small island (once thick with fish) had been hammered by dynamite, poisoned with cyanide, pounded by rocks. Catches kept dropping. Fishermen kept working longer hours, using more destructive methods, which made things worse.

One of many stories of communities who reversed ecological collapse, drawn on throughout the Ingredients for Success and Systems Thinking tools.

Then in 1982, fourteen families did something counterintuitive: they stopped fishing in 10% of their fishing grounds.

Three years later, fish were spilling out of that protected area in numbers they hadn't seen in decades. Within fifteen years, their catch per hour had more than tripled. They were fishing right at home again, working fewer hours, and their children were going to university.

More than 700 other Philippine fishing villages have since tried versions of what Apo Island figured out. Some succeeded. Some didn't. The difference came down to whether they understood what Apo Island understood: you can't fix a system by working harder within it. You have to change what's driving the system itself.

### How It Happened

Apo Island is 78 hectares of volcanic rock nine kilometers off the coast of Negros. About 700 people live there, most of them fishing from small outrigger canoes in the coral reefs that ring the island. Until the 1960s, this worked. Fishermen used hook-and-line, gill nets, bamboo traps: methods that had sustained their parents and grandparents. The reefs were healthy and fish were plentiful enough that a few hours of work fed a family.

Then things changed, though not on Apo Island specifically. Across the Philippines, four destructive fishing practices spread after World War II. Dynamite fishing started with leftover explosives and became common by the 1960s. Muro-ami (pounding coral with rocks to chase fish into nets) came from Japan. Cyanide fishing arrived in the 1970s, initially for the aquarium trade. And small-mesh nylon nets, newly available worldwide, made it easy to catch fish before they could reproduce.

These methods worked better than traditional ones, at least in the short term. They were also illegal by the early 1980s, but enforcement across thousands of islands and coastlines was impossible. The methods spread because they had to: when fish stocks dropped, fishermen needed more effective techniques to catch what remained.

What happened next was predictable. Blast fishing destroyed coral structure. Cyanide killed everything it touched, not just target species. Small-mesh nets caught juvenile fish before they could reproduce. As reefs degraded and fish populations crashed, fishermen had no choice but to use these methods even more intensively. Some areas of the Philippines saw fish stocks drop to 5-10% of what they'd been fifty years earlier. In bad areas, a full day's work might yield one or two small fish. Or none.

On Apo Island, fishermen started making longer trips, sometimes 10 kilometers out. They'd leave before dawn, work into darkness, and still barely make enough to eat. The time away from home meant less attention to protecting local waters, weaker community cohesion and less enforcement of informal rules. Moving outside of their own fishing grounds also gave fishermen less incentive to protect them. Which meant more destructive fishing, which made things worse.

Everyone knew what was happening. The older fishermen remembered when things were different. But knowing doesn't fix it when you're the only one who stops using dynamite while everyone else keeps blasting. Individual virtue doesn't work against collective action problems.

#### The Turning Point

In 1979, Angel Alcala showed up. He ran the marine lab at Silliman University and had helped establish a no-fishing sanctuary at uninhabited Sumilon Island in 1974. By 1979, Sumilon was packed with fish. Alcala wanted to see if the same approach could work where people actually lived and fished.

He started talking with Apo fishermen about what was happening to their reefs. The conversations went on for three years. He took some of them to see Sumilon; they could see the fish, see how the protected area was restocking the surrounding waters. But the approach didn't make intuitive sense. You don't stop fishing when you don't have enough fish. And nobody else in the Philippines was doing this. Why would Apo Island be first?

In 1982, fourteen families decided to try it anyway. They had the support of the Apo Island administrative leader, the barangay captain. This mattered, because in a community of 145 households, you can't do something this unusual without local government backing. They picked a stretch of shoreline: 450 meters long, extending 500 meters out. About 10% of their fishing grounds. The area had some healthy coral but not many fish.

Protecting it turned out to be straightforward. One person watching from the beach, rotating among the families. That was it.

By 1985, you could see the difference. Fish were bigger, more numerous. And they weren't staying in the sanctuary; they were spilling over into the areas people fished. Catches near the sanctuary edges got better.

#### Expanding the System

Once that happened, skepticism evaporated. If protecting 10% worked this well, what about managing all the fishing grounds around the island? They brought in help from a coastal management nonprofit and set up a Marine Management Committee. When big decisions needed making, representatives from every family gathered at the school playground. They talked until everyone agreed, however long that took.

They decided on two rules: no destructive fishing anywhere, and only Apo Island fishermen could fish their waters. The second rule was unprecedented: it required negotiating with higher government levels for authority they didn't normally have. They got it.

They created a volunteer marine guard, bantay dagat, to enforce the rules. Mostly this meant checking boats from other areas. They didn't need to police their own fishermen anymore; the sanctuary had become sacred, and sustainable fishing had become what you did if you were from Apo Island.

When fishermen went back to traditional methods (hooks, gill nets, bamboo traps), the coral and fish stocks came back. Not immediately, but the ecosystem still had enough of its original biodiversity to heal once people stopped hammering it. By the mid-1990s, catch per hour had more than tripled.

Fishermen didn't catch more fish total. They caught the same amount in less time, then stopped fishing. A few hours work fed the family and covered necessities. Some used the extra time for other income: ferrying supplies or people to the mainland. Mostly they used extra income for their kids' education.

#### Virtuous Cycles

Then other things started happening. Divers started showing up: the reefs around the entire island were now spectacular, full of fish and turtles and sea snakes. Tour boats came from the mainland. Someone opened a dive shop. Two small hotels. Families started taking in boarders, women catered for the hotels, sold t-shirts and sarongs. Women's associations formed. The island government collected diving fees and used the money for the school, garbage collection, water, electricity.

The hotel owner started giving scholarships. More than half the island's kids could now afford high school on the mainland. Many went to university. Some studied marine ecosystem management and came back as professionals: teachers, health workers, resource managers. The community's capacity to handle whatever came next kept growing.

That mattered, because tourism brought new problems. Too many divers were damaging the coral. The community already knew how to handle this; they'd been managing the sanctuary and fishing grounds for years. They restricted tourist numbers in the sanctuary, banned divers from coming within 50 meters of fishing activity, made the prime fishing area completely off-limits.

#### Spreading the Model

Other villages noticed. Apo's barangay captain started visiting other fishing communities to explain how it worked. People came to Apo to see for themselves. In 1994, when Alcala became Minister of Natural Resources, the Philippine government launched a national marine sanctuary network. About 700 sanctuaries exist now. Not all work well; success depended on whether communities actually followed Apo's model rather than just putting up a sign. But many are on the same path.

The national network helped but also complicated things; Apo lost some autonomy over sanctuary management and how tourist fees got used. There are tensions with higher levels of government now that didn't exist when they were just figuring it out themselves.

Still, the island works. People aren't rich but they're not desperate. They value what they have and they're committed to keeping it. The sanctuary taught them something: you have to change things to keep things the same. Recently they started a family planning program with help from a local nonprofit, making sure future population doesn't outstrip what the fishing grounds can provide.

The sanctuary is sacred to people on Apo Island. They say it saved their reef, their fishery, their way of life. It's unthinkable to violate it. The power of that shared belief (whether literally true or a story the community tells itself) has proven essential to maintaining the restoration.

##### Virtuous Cycle: The Restoration

### How the System Changed

Recognizing it started with three years of conversation. Angel Alcala didn't arrive at Apo Island with a plan; he arrived with a question about what was happening to the reef, and he kept coming back. Taking fishermen to see the Sumilon sanctuary wasn't a sales pitch; it was evidence. By 1982, enough of the community understood the feedback loops driving their decline (damaged reefs meant fewer fish meant more destructive fishing meant more damaged reefs, and fishing farther from home meant weaker local enforcement meant more local destruction) that fourteen families were willing to try something counterintuitive.

Mapping it happened through the community's own decision-making process. Once the sanctuary proved itself, they didn't just protect it; they mapped what managing all their fishing grounds would require. Representatives from every family gathered at the school playground. Two rules emerged from those discussions: no destructive fishing anywhere, and only Apo Island fishermen in Apo Island waters. Simple enough to enforce, comprehensive enough to address root causes.

Reversing it hinged on the sanctuary being a genuine leverage point. Ten percent of their fishing grounds: large enough that fish recovery was substantial, positioned where spillover into fishing areas was visible within three years. That speed mattered. The same people who made the decision got to see it work before community commitment could erode.

Locking it in happened through accumulating virtuous cycles. Recovering fish stocks built management experience and pride, which strengthened protection, which further recovered stocks. Tourism funded infrastructure, which improved quality of life, which deepened commitment to the reef. Education produced professionals who came back as resource managers, which improved adaptive capacity. Each cycle reinforced the others.

The model also spread, not through top-down mandate but because other villages could see what Apo had done and decide to try it themselves. When Alcala became Minister of Natural Resources in 1994, he helped formalize a national network of over 700 sanctuaries, but the spread had already begun through fishermen visiting fishermen. Success at one location became the template for success elsewhere. Healthy coral reefs also store significant carbon in their skeletons and living biomass; Apo's local recovery contributed to carbon sequestration at scale in ways the community never set out to achieve.

### Key Ingredients

All ten ingredients for success appear in this story. A few shaped the outcome most directly.

Outside stimulation and facilitation opened the door, but on the community's terms. Alcala spent three years in dialogue before anyone acted. He provided evidence and kept showing up; he didn't implement the sanctuary. The university scientists made the approach thinkable; the fourteen families and the barangay captain made it real.

Shared community awareness and commitment came through process, not persuasion. Getting representatives from every family to consensus (however long it took, with roast pork and everything else that made the gathering work) meant everyone had ownership of the rules they'd be living under. That ownership is what made enforcement function without coercion.

Harmony between community and ecosystem was built deliberately. The community organization they created (the Marine Management Committee, the bantay dagat, the family representative assemblies) was specifically designed to fit the ecology they were managing. Social commons matched environmental commons. That fit is why the management held when outside attention moved on.

Letting nature do the work was the core mechanism. Protecting 10% of fishing grounds and stopping destructive practices gave the ecosystem room to heal itself. The coral still had enough of its original biodiversity to regenerate; nature did the restoration work once people stopped preventing it.

Overcoming social obstacles required solving a genuine collective action problem. Before the sanctuary, every individual fisherman was rationally incentivized to keep using destructive methods; stopping unilaterally while competitors kept blasting made no sense. The sanctuary only became viable when the community created assurance that everyone would stop together. That coordination, not individual virtue, broke the cycle.

Mobilizing community commitment through powerful symbols and rapid results sustained it through the uncertain years. Three years to visible fish recovery was fast enough. And when the sanctuary became sacred (literally unthinkable to violate) the behavioral change stopped depending on enforcement and became self-sustaining.

Social and ecological memory shaped even the sanctuary's location. The community chose a stretch of reef that older fishermen remembered as historically productive. That memory (of what the ecosystem had been) informed where intervention would have the most recovery potential.

Building resilience showed up most clearly when success created new problems. When tourism volumes started damaging coral, the community drew on years of fishery management experience to impose restrictions on divers, even at the cost of short-term income. Women's associations, income diversification from tourism, and the island government's ability to fund infrastructure from diving fees all added layers of resilience that didn't exist when the story started.

# Case Study: Gopalpura, India

Case Study

## Gopalpura, India

How a Dried-Up Village Learned to Bank Water

In 1985, the wells in Gopalpura had been dry for years. Women and children spent up to ten hours a day walking to fetch water and firewood. Men had left for the cities. The streams were gone. So were the leopards and antelopes that had once characterized the landscape. An entire region of Rajasthan had pumped its underground water dry, and nothing in the modern toolkit seemed able to reverse it.

One of many stories of communities who reversed ecological collapse, drawn on throughout the Ingredients for Success and Systems Thinking tools.

Then five young volunteers arrived in the village intending to start a health clinic. A village elder named Mangu Patel redirected them. What Gopalpura needed, he said, wasn't medicine. It was water.

They started digging.

### How It Happened

#### A Thousand Years, Then Gone

Water has always been scarce in Alwar District. The monsoon delivers about 40 centimeters of rain across three months. For the rest of the year, the land is dry. Over millennia, farmers developed an answer: the johad, a crescent-shaped earthen dam built to intercept rainwater flowing down slopes. On the surface, a johad held water for livestock. Below the surface was its real function: holding water in place long enough for it to percolate down through soil and recharge the aquifer, sometimes as far as a kilometer away. Wells stayed full. Dry-season irrigation was possible. A johad was more than any family could build alone, so villages built and maintained them together.

This system held for centuries. It unraveled fast.

When India approached independence in the late 1940s, the local raja, fearing land seizure by the new national government, sold logging rights to commercial companies. The ancient forests came down. Without tree cover, topsoil washed off hillsides and silted up the johads. The traditional village councils that had organized communal johad maintenance (gram sabhas with participation from every family) had already been weakened by British colonial policies that eroded communal land rights and replaced shared governance with individual competition. With less incentive to protect shared resources and less institutional capacity to do so, johads fell into disrepair.

The introduction of tube well drilling equipment in the 1950s made the johads seem obsolete anyway. A dry well just needed to go deeper. This was easier than the labor of clearing johad sediment, and it worked, for a while. Vicious cycles accelerated the collapse: deeper wells sucked more water from the aquifer, dropping the water table further, which killed trees and vegetation whose roots had reached underground water, which increased erosion, which silted up the remaining johads faster. Less vegetation meant less transpiration, which meant shorter monsoons: 101 days in 1973, 55 days by 1987. Eventually the aquifer was exhausted over thousands of square kilometers. Wells went dry. Then streams. Then rivers.

#### One Dam, One Monsoon

Rajendra Singh had come to Gopalpura with TBS (Tarun Bharat Sangh, a young people's anti-poverty organization) to open a health clinic. Mangu Patel's redirection changed the trajectory of the entire region.

Singh and his colleagues started excavating a silted johad. Seven months of digging, nearly five meters deep. When the monsoon arrived, the pond filled. A nearby well that had been dry for years began flowing again.

The village had its answer. The following year, the whole community joined to rebuild a crumbling irrigation dam: 20 feet high, 1,400 feet long, and built on 10,000 person-days of voluntary labor. They also revived the gram sabha alongside the modern village council, because the gram sabha's total-participation, consensus-based structure was what large communal construction required. Wealthier villagers donated food to feed the workers. TBS provided technical assistance.

By 1996, Gopalpura had nine johads covering 964 hectares and holding up to 616 million liters of water. The underground water table rose from 14 meters below ground to 6.7 meters. Every well in the village was full.

#### The Aquifer as Bank

"It's like a bank," Rajendra Singh says. "If you make regular deposits, then you'll always have money to withdraw. If you are just taking, then you'll have no money in your bank account."

The metaphor describes exactly what happened. As water returned, the village economy rose with it. Moister subsoil meant crops needed less irrigation to thrive, and restored wells provided what irrigation was needed. Diesel fuel costs for pumping dropped 75 percent. Wheat fields expanded from 33 to 108 hectares. Farmers diversified into sugarcane, potatoes, and onions. Men came back from the cities, bringing labor for more johad construction. More johads meant more water, which sustained more trees, which protected soil from erosion, which kept the johads from silting up. The mirror image of the collapse.

Women whose days had been consumed by fetching water and firewood formed cooperatives selling dairy products, handicrafts, and soap. Girls started going to school for the first time. Villagers reforested 10 hectares at the village edge, wrote conservation rules for the new forest, and tied rakhis (kinship bracelets) around the trees as a symbol of family protection.

#### Spreading Across the Region

As other villages witnessed Gopalpura's transformation, they came asking for help. TBS would assist, but only after each village signed a formal contract committing the money and labor they would contribute. This requirement wasn't bureaucratic; it was the lesson Gopalpura had taught. Johads that communities hadn't built themselves didn't get maintained.

TBS publicized the johad revival through foot marches, a practice drawn from Indian spiritual tradition and Gandhi's independence movement. Thousands of people walked through the region. Thousands more visited Gopalpura. By 2005, there were 5,000 johads across 750 villages covering 8,000 square kilometers. A survey of 970 wells found all of them flowing, including 800 that had been dry just six years before. Five rivers came back to life. Forest cover in Alwar District expanded by 33 percent in 15 years.

The government of Rajasthan tried at various points to assert control, threatening TBS workers with arrest for "taking the law into their own hands," sending officials to tear down johads, selling commercial fishing rights to the reborn Arvari River. Villages resisted, sometimes by physically blocking government action. Seventy villages united to reclaim the fishing rights. Residents of the Sariska Tiger Reserve successfully sued to expel illegal marble mining operations that were draining and contaminating their underground water. The communities that had learned to build johads had also learned to defend them.

### How the System Changed

Recognizing it happened through a conversation rather than a study. Mangu Patel didn't need a systems analysis to know what Gopalpura needed; the wells were dry and the women were walking ten hours a day. What TBS brought was the connection between that visible crisis and its less-visible cause: a centuries-old water management system had been dismantled, piece by piece, and no modern substitute was working. Naming that clearly was the precondition for the solution.

Mapping it revealed a cascade that traced back to deforestation. Logging drove erosion, which silted johads, which weakened the aquifer, which prompted deeper tube wells, which dropped the water table further, which killed vegetation, which increased erosion. The map also showed where the feedback could be reversed: the johad was the pivot point. Restore water retention and the whole sequence could run in the other direction.

Reversing it started with one dam in one village, timed to one monsoon. The proof was visible within months: a dry well flowing again. That immediacy mattered enormously in a community that had been told for decades that modern solutions like deeper wells were the answer. Something as ancient and simple as an earthen dam outperformed the machinery.

Locking it in required rebuilding the social infrastructure as much as the physical. The gram sabha revival wasn't incidental; it was what made sustained construction possible. Johads are communal assets that require communal maintenance. Recreating the governance structure that had built them originally was the mechanism that made the restoration permanent. And TBS's insistence that each new village contribute labor and resources before receiving help ensured that communities owned what they built. By 2005, the johad network was too large, too socially embedded, and too legally defended to be undone.

### Key Ingredients

Social and ecological memory was the foundation of everything. The johad wasn't invented; it was remembered. Village elders knew how to build them and where to put them. The gram sabha tradition of consensus-based communal governance hadn't disappeared; it had been suppressed. TBS didn't introduce a foreign technology; it helped a community recover knowledge it already possessed.

Outside stimulation and facilitation arrived aimed at something else entirely. TBS came for health and education. That responsiveness to what the community actually needed was itself a lesson. Rajendra Singh listened to Mangu Patel. The organization followed the village's lead, then stayed for decades providing technical assistance and connecting villages to one another.

Letting nature do the work was the elegance of the johad. The dam did nothing but hold water in place long enough for gravity and soil to do the rest. No pipes, no pumps, no infrastructure beyond an earthen wall. Nature transported water from dam to well underground, at no cost, across distances no pipe system could have served affordably.

Shared community awareness and commitment was institutionalized rather than assumed. The gram sabha revival meant that johad decisions (where to build, how to maintain, who contributed what) were made by the whole community, in meetings that ran for days if necessary, until genuine consensus emerged. That process was slow. It was also what made maintenance happen without enforcement.

Harmony between community and ecosystem deepened as the restoration progressed. The rebounding aquifer sustained trees. Trees protected soil. Protected soil kept johads clear. Clear johads recharged the aquifer. Communities that understood this feedback loop, and had built the institutions to manage it, became progressively more capable of maintaining it.

Enduring commitment of local leadership spanned decades. Rajendra Singh and TBS didn't complete the Gopalpura project and move on. They hosted the thousands of visitors who came to learn, helped new villages establish their own gram sabhas, and stood with communities when governments tried to reverse the gains. Leadership that stays the course through the long middle of restoration, after the first dramatic results but before the work is secure, is what this story turns on.

Community partner: Gopalpura Village, Alwar District, Rajasthan, India; Tarun Bharat Sangh

# Case Study: Khao Din, Thailand

Case Study

## Khao Din, Thailand

When Farming the Forest Brought the Forest Back

By the late 1980s, farmers in Khao Din had cut down every tree they could find. They had to; debt demanded it. The timber money helped, briefly. Then the soil started washing away, the streams dried up, and the crops kept failing no matter how many chemicals they applied. In a village that had arrived decades earlier to find dense jungle full of fish and wild boar, people were going hungry.

One of many stories of communities who reversed ecological collapse, drawn on throughout the Ingredients for Success and Systems Thinking tools.

In 1988, a team from Save the Children arrived. They didn't bring aid. They brought a question: how did this happen?

### How It Happened

#### The Downward Spiral

When Ajaan Thanawm's family migrated to Nakhon Sawan province in 1954, they found what felt like abundance: thick forest, fertile soil, streams full of fish, food growing wild near the houses. About fifty families settled there. For years, small-scale farming sustained them.

Then in the 1960s, the Thai government, backed by World Bank development models, pushed rural farmers toward export cash crops. An Agricultural Credit Bank extended loans for hybrid seed, chemical fertilizers, pesticides, and equipment. Farmers switched from diversified traditional agriculture to monocropping. They cut more forest to expand fields. For a while, money flowed.

It didn't last. As thousands of farmers grew the same crops, prices fell. Droughts came. Harvests failed. Unable to repay the Agricultural Bank, farmers fell into the hands of informal lenders charging 10% interest per month. Desperate to service their debts, they cut the last remaining trees. With no forest cover, the hillsides baked. Soil lost its capacity to hold moisture. Streams dried up. Chemical costs climbed as soil fertility collapsed, requiring more fertilizer to produce less food.

Eventually families couldn't grow enough to eat. People left for Bangkok. Communities that had been tightly knit fractured. "Everyone was now worried about their own fields and their own family's problems," Thanawm recalls. "For the first time ever, we began to have psychological and social problems. There was little trust and less cooperation." Juvenile delinquency (previously unheard of) appeared. The village had gone from near-abundance to hunger and social disintegration in a single generation.

#### The Turning Point

The Save the Children team sent to Khao Din in 1986 did something unusual: instead of delivering outside solutions, they helped villagers understand what had happened to them. Through long, sometimes difficult conversations, farmers came to see the feedback loops driving their decline: how debt led to deforestation led to soil collapse led to more debt. "It was amazing! People were lighting up like light bulbs!" recalls team member Andrew Mittelman. "They kept saying: 'My God, what have we done? We couldn't imagine this place of abundance would become a desert.'"

That recognition (collective, not individual) was the turning point. And it contained its own momentum: if the community had made decisions that caused this, they could make decisions that reversed it.

#### Rebuilding from the Ground Up

The farmers and Save the Children staff began experimenting with agroforestry, mixing trees and crops on the same land in ways that resembled the structure of a natural forest. It wasn't a foreign concept. Their traditional subsistence farming had incorporated many of the same elements. What was different now was the intentionality.

Thanawm was among the first to try it. He planted fruit trees along the periphery of his farm, dug a narrow fish pond with a vegetable island in the middle, and filled the surrounding plots with a rotating mix of fruits, vegetables, medicinal plants, and herbs. The result was a farm that produced food year-round without chemicals, because the diversity of the system did the work that chemicals had been trying to do: natural pest control, organic fertilizer from leaf fall, moisture retention from dense vegetation, soil protection from erosion.

The demonstration worked visibly enough that neighbors adopted similar approaches. As more farms converted, the aggregate effect on the landscape became apparent: water returned to some streams, soil stabilized, food security improved dramatically.

The community also established a community forest on shared land. They wrote rules governing harvest of forest resources and excluded outsiders from cutting. As the forest recovered, animals long thought locally extinct reappeared. So did a sense of collective ownership: of the land, and of the community's ability to shape its own future.

#### What Compounded

After Save the Children left, Thanawm kept going. He founded the Association of Agriculture, the Environment and Development, Nakhon Sawan, which grew to include 40 villages and 2,545 families practicing locally-adapted versions of agroforestry across thousands of acres. In the late 1990s, the Thai government's "Sufficiency Economy" program (championed by the King) began supporting this approach nationally, giving the community's model institutional recognition it hadn't sought but could use.

The restored forest also turned out to matter beyond the village. Tropical deforestation accounts for 17% of global carbon dioxide emissions. Khao Din's reforestation sequestered carbon at a meaningful scale.

Today Khao Din has 2,500 residents and migration to Bangkok has reversed. The village doesn't generate large cash incomes, and younger generations feel that tension. But the community has something that's hard to rebuild once lost: agency. "We know now that with some careful thinking and a lot of shared effort, we can solve our problems, and fix what is broken," says Thanawm. "Even though we don't have much money, I'm happy."

### How the System Changed

Recognizing it started with the Save the Children team's facilitated conversations in 1986. Farmers traced the history of their decline, not as victims of circumstance, but as people who had made choices that triggered reinforcing loops: debt led to deforestation led to soil collapse led to more debt. That shared diagnosis was itself the turning point. Farmers who understood the feedback loops driving their situation could design their way out. Farmers who didn't would have kept applying more chemicals to failing soil.

Mapping it happened through collective problem-solving. The community worked backward through the vicious cycles: export crop dependence, chemical costs, deforestation, erosion, declining yields, more debt, identifying where the loops could be broken. The answer wasn't a single intervention but an integrated shift: agroforestry to restore soil and food security, community forest management to protect the watershed, and a gradual conversion process that didn't require farmers to risk everything at once.

Reversing it began with demonstration plots. Thanawm and a few other willing farmers tried agroforestry first, on land their neighbors could observe. Results were tangible within a single growing season: more food, no chemicals, visible improvement. That made adoption a choice based on evidence rather than a leap of faith.

Locking it in happened through accumulation. Debt paid off. Streams returned. Families stopped leaving for Bangkok. Thanawm founded a regional association that brought the approach to 40 villages. The Thai government's Sufficiency Economy program eventually gave institutional backing to what the community had already proven. And the restored forest began sequestering carbon at scale.

### Key Ingredients

All ten ingredients for success appear in this story, but a few are worth highlighting given how prominently they shaped the outcome.

Outside stimulation and facilitation was essential, but time-limited by design. Save the Children's most important contribution wasn't technical knowledge; it was facilitating collective diagnosis. They left. The community continued.

Shared community awareness and commitment preceded everything else. The "lighting up like light bulbs" moment Mittelman describes wasn't inspiration; it was recognition. Farmers who understood what had happened to their land could own the solution in a way that outside-designed interventions never produce.

Letting nature do the work was the core mechanism of agroforestry. The system succeeded because it mimicked forest structure, allowing ecological processes to restore what chemicals had been trying to replace: soil fertility, moisture retention, pest balance. The community stopped fighting natural systems and put them to work.

Social and ecological memory gave the approach its foundation. Agroforestry wasn't foreign; it resembled the traditional farming these families had practiced before the export crop push. Older villagers remembered what the landscape had looked like. That memory made the new approach feel like recovery rather than experiment.

Enduring commitment of local leadership sustained it after the outside catalyst left. Thanawm kept organizing when there was no institutional support, no funding, and no guarantee the regional network would hold. He did it anyway, and it grew.

Research and documentation: Dr. Gerald G. Marten, Amanda Suutari, EcoTipping Points Project

Community partner: Khao Din Village, Nakhon Sawan Province, Thailand

# Case Study: Punukula, India

Case Study

## Punukula, India

Escaping the Pesticide Trap

The suicide notes from Andhra Pradesh farmers often mentioned the same thing: debt. By the late 1990s, the state had the highest farmer suicide rate in India. The method of choice was an insecticide cocktail, the same chemicals that had trapped them in debt in the first place.

One of many stories of communities who reversed ecological collapse, drawn on throughout the Ingredients for Success and Systems Thinking tools.

In the village of Punukula, a community of about 900 people, one family's crisis became the turning point. When a prominent elder's son collapsed from acute pesticide poisoning and spent a week unconscious, the hospital bill pushed the family's debt to a breaking point. The elder, Margam Mutthaiah, decided he had nothing left to lose by trying something different.

Within two years, every farmer in Punukula had stopped using chemical pesticides. Within a decade, 340,000 farmers across 3,170 villages had followed the same path.

### How It Happened

#### The Trap

Cotton arrived in Andhra Pradesh in the early 1980s with the promise of cash income. Small-scale farmers who had grown millet, sorghum, peanuts, and vegetables for home consumption and local sale saw an opportunity. Growing cotton required pesticides and chemical fertilizers, inputs these mostly illiterate farmers had never used before. For guidance, they relied on local agrochemical dealers who sold seeds, chemicals, and fertilizers on credit and offered advice supplied by the multinational companies behind the products.

The early years worked. Yields were strong, debt was manageable, money was new. Then the pests arrived (bollworms, leafworms, aphids) and the chemistry that had seemed so powerful started losing the battle. Repeated spraying killed the most susceptible insects and left the resistant ones to reproduce. As resistance spread through pest populations, farmers sprayed more often and mixed more chemicals, sometimes combining ten different insecticides in a single application. The spraying killed the birds, wasps, beetles, and spiders that had previously controlled pests naturally. With those predators gone, cutting back on chemicals meant losing the crop entirely.

The trap closed in every direction. Pesticide costs escalated as chemical fertilizers became necessary to compensate for soil stripped of natural fertility. The cost of producing cotton exceeded what cotton sold for. Debt compounded. Dealers who held the debt warned farmers: stop buying chemicals and repay everything immediately. Children went into indentured labor to service family debts. Education stopped. Everyone on the farm, including children, sprayed toxic chemicals by hand, and chronic headaches, nausea, skin problems, and neurological damage became ordinary facts of life.

#### One Farmer, One Season

Around 1998, a nonprofit worker named Karnati Venu Madhav began talking to Punukula farmers about Non-Pesticide Management (NPM), a pest control approach developed by the Center for Sustainable Agriculture using neem and other natural methods. Madhav had grown up in a farming family with the same problems. His organization, SECURE, arranged for some villagers to travel 400 kilometers to see a farm where NPM was actually working. They saw it with their own eyes. They remained skeptical.

Then Mutthaiah's son nearly died. The hospital bill was the last straw. Mutthaiah, a respected village elder with deep agricultural debt, agreed to try NPM on his fields. SECURE coached him through it.

Neem, a common tree throughout India, was the foundation. Its natural compounds interfere with insect feeding, growth, and egg production without harming birds, predatory insects, or humans. Seeds are ground to powder, soaked in water, and sprayed every ten days. Neem cake applied to soil doubles as organic fertilizer. The only cost is labor. Complementing the neem: chili-garlic spray for severe infestations, cow dung and urine mixture to deter sucking insects, a naturally occurring virus that kills bollworms, trap crops planted at field margins to draw pests away from cotton, sticky boards for whiteflies, bonfires on moonless nights to attract moths, bird perches to enlist natural predators. Deep summer plowing to disrupt pest life cycles in the soil. Vermicompost to rebuild soil fertility without chemicals.

Mutthaiah's harvest was as good as his neighbors' who were still spraying, and he hadn't spent money on insecticides. Twenty farmers tried NPM the following season. SECURE stationed two staff members in the village, a man and a woman, to teach and assist. Women pushed their husbands to stop using the chemicals that were making their families sick. The results held. The year after that, more farmers converted.

By 2000, every farmer in Punukula used NPM on cotton. In 2004, the village council formally declared Punukula pesticide-free and extended vermicomposting across the village, eliminating the need for chemical fertilizers entirely.

#### What Came Back

Pest predator populations recovered once the chemicals stopped. As birds, wasps, and beetles returned, even neem applications became less necessary; nature reasserted its own control. Medical expenses dropped. Debt became payable. When dealers tried to demand immediate full repayment, the village negotiated collectively and won.

Freed indentured children returned to school through special catch-up courses. Women who had gathered and processed neem for their own farms began selling neem and other NPM materials to villages across the region, a new income source and a new source of standing. The village set up a marketing cooperative when dealers tried to underpay for NPM cotton. They lobbied government. They built a small cotton ginning operation. They purified their drinking water. The capacity to fight pesticide companies and win turned out to be transferable to every other problem they faced.

The suicide epidemic ended.

Punukula became a destination. Thousands of farmers from other villages came to see what had happened. Multinational pesticide corporations lobbied the state government to suppress NPM. The government did the opposite, incorporating NPM into its statewide rural development program and disseminating it through women's self-help groups. By 2008, 340,000 farmers across 3,170 villages were using NPM on nearly a million acres, not just cotton but grain and vegetable crops too.

### How the System Changed

Recognizing it began with the visit to a working NPM farm 400 kilometers away. Seeing wasn't enough to overcome skepticism, but it established that escape was possible. What actually broke through was a personal crisis: Mutthaiah's son's poisoning made the cost of staying in the system undeniable in a way that abstract argument hadn't. SECURE's role was to be present, patient, and ready when a community member was ready to try.

Mapping it had already been done, in a sense, by the Center for Sustainable Agriculture. The feedback loops were well understood: pesticide use killed natural predators, which increased pest pressure, which required more pesticides, which increased resistance, which required even more pesticides. The map pointed clearly to the lever: restore natural predator populations by eliminating chemicals, and the system would begin to correct itself.

Reversing it required solving a collective action problem first. Individual farmers couldn't switch to NPM safely while neighbors continued spraying; chemicals drift, pests migrate, and a single unprotected field is vulnerable. SECURE's presence in the village enabled the coordinated conversion that made individual risk manageable. When enough farmers switched together, the critical mass of natural predator recovery made the whole approach more viable for everyone.

Locking it in happened on multiple fronts simultaneously. The village council's formal pesticide-free declaration in 2004 made the change institutional. The women's neem supply network created economic stakes in NPM's continuation. The marketing cooperative meant farmers no longer depended on the dealers who had trapped them. Each new enterprise (cotton ginning, water purification, the cooperative) added another layer of independence from the systems that had made them vulnerable.

The spread beyond Punukula came through visibility and government alignment. Visitors came, saw, and carried the model home. When the state government chose to promote rather than suppress NPM, the infrastructure of women's self-help groups provided a distribution network that no nonprofit could have built. Local success became state policy, which became one of the largest pesticide reduction programs in the world.

### Key Ingredients

Outside stimulation and facilitation was the ignition, not the engine. SECURE provided technical training, arranged the critical site visit, and kept staff present in the village long enough for adoption to reach critical mass. But the organization's most important act was helping farmers understand a system they were already living inside, giving them the conceptual tools to see the trap as a trap.

Letting nature do the work was literal here. NPM succeeded because it restored the ecological relationships that chemical farming had destroyed. Once predator populations recovered, the system's own dynamics shifted in favor of the farmers. Neem worked because it had evolved over millions of years to defeat plant-eating insects; human ingenuity borrowed what nature had already designed.

Social and ecological memory made the transition thinkable. NPM wasn't a foreign technology; it was a modern, systematic version of traditional farming that preceded chemical agriculture. Neem had been used in India for centuries. The knowledge of how to farm without chemicals hadn't vanished entirely; it needed to be recovered and organized.

Overcoming social obstacles required collective action that individual farmers couldn't achieve alone. The dealer debt system was designed to make exit impossible. SECURE's sustained presence made coordinated conversion feasible. The village's united front against dealer pressure, negotiating collectively rather than each family facing intimidation alone, was what made debt resolution possible.

Shared community awareness and commitment was built as much by women as by male farmers. Women had been spraying chemicals alongside their husbands and children. They understood what the chemicals were doing to their families' health. Their pressure on reluctant husbands to adopt NPM, and their subsequent role in producing and distributing NPM materials across the region, turned a farming technique into a community transformation.

Building resilience compounded with each new initiative. Debt clearance improved financial resilience. Diverse income streams (neem sales, the cooperative, cotton ginning, tourism from visiting farmers) reduced vulnerability to any single point of failure. The political confidence that came from confronting and defeating multinational corporations made the community harder to exploit in the future. What started as a farming technique became the foundation of a community that knew how to organize, negotiate, and win.

Research and documentation: Dr. Gerald G. Marten, EcoTipping Points Project

Community partner: Punukula Village, Andhra Pradesh, India; Center for Sustainable Agriculture, Hyderabad
